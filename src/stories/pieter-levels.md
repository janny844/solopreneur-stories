# Pieter Levels: The Nomad Who Built 12 Startups in 12 Months

The flight from Bangkok to Chiang Mai takes fifty minutes.

Pieter Levels spent that flight writing code.

Not planning code. Not sketching on a napkin. Writing it — in a middle seat, on a 13-inch MacBook Air, with the tray table down and his elbows tucked against his sides because the man next to him was asleep and taking half the armrest.

This was 2014. He had been traveling for four months. He had launched two startups. Both had failed. He had $4,400 left in his account, no permanent address, and a public promise on his website that he intended to keep: twelve startups in twelve months.

He had ten left.

---

## The Bet He Made With Himself

Pieter Levels is Dutch. He grew up in the Netherlands, studied at a university, played in a band, started a YouTube channel that reached three million subscribers before he shut it down because it felt like a job.

The pattern was already there, if you knew to look for it: he would build something, it would work, and then he would leave.

Not because things got hard. Because things got normal.

In 2013, he sold everything he owned — the furniture, the guitar, most of his clothes — and bought a one-way ticket to Asia. He was twenty-six. His plan was to travel indefinitely and fund it through online work.

The twelve startups idea came later, from frustration. He had too many ideas and not enough urgency. The public commitment was a forcing function — a way of saying: if you're going to build these things, build them now, because you've told the entire internet you would.

The internet held him to it.

---

## The Product That Worked

Startup number four was called Nomad List.

It started as a spreadsheet. A simple Google Sheets document where Pieter logged the cities he'd been to: cost of living, internet speed, weather, safety score. He made it public because sharing felt natural.

Within a week, 10,000 people had viewed it.

He turned it into a website in a weekend. No landing page, no investor pitch, no user research sessions. Just a database of cities and a simple interface to sort them. He launched it on Hacker News with a two-sentence post.

It got to the front page.

The traffic crashed his $5/month server three times that day. He upgraded the server between each crash. By the end of the week, Nomad List had 800,000 pageviews and Pieter had made $0 from it.

He added a $100/year membership the following Monday.

He made $1,000 in the first hour.

---

## The Architecture of One Person

Here is what Pieter Levels understood before most people did: the internet makes it possible for one person to serve a million people, but only if you build the product to require zero people.

Every feature he didn't build was a support ticket that would never arrive. Every decision he automated was a morning he didn't spend in his inbox. He built Nomad List to run without him not because he was lazy, but because he was one person and there was a finite amount of him to go around.

He did not hire a team. He did not raise funding. Investors emailed. He ignored them.

"If I take investment, I have to do what the investor wants," he said in an interview. "I don't want to do what investors want. I want to do what I want."

What he wanted, specifically, was to work four hours a day, travel wherever he liked, and build products that solved real problems for real people.

He made $2.7 million in revenue in 2023. Solo. From a beach in Thailand.

---

## The Twelve Months

He finished the challenge.

Twelve startups. Not twelve successes — twelve launches. Some made money. Most didn't. One, Remote OK, grew alongside Nomad List into a job board that served 100,000 monthly users.

The point was never to succeed twelve times. The point was to ship twelve times — to build the muscle memory of launching, of putting things in front of people, of seeing what happened when you stopped perfecting and started publishing.

"Every time I ship something and it doesn't work," he said, "I learn something worth more than if it had worked."

He would know.

---

## What He Carried Forward

The question people always ask Pieter Levels is: how do you stay motivated without a team, without an office, without the structure of a normal company?

He gives the same answer every time.

"I'm not motivated," he says. "I'm curious."

Motivation is emotional — it rises and falls with mood, sleep, weather. Curiosity is structural. It pulls you toward problems because you genuinely want to know what happens when you try to solve them.

The fifty-minute flight from Bangkok to Chiang Mai is long enough to build a feature if you already know what you're building. The trick is always knowing what you're building next.

He has never not known.

---

## The Number

Pieter Levels's publicly disclosed revenue is approximately $3 million per year.

One person. No office. No team. A laptop that lives in a backpack, a collection of small products that compound slowly into something large, and a calendar with no commute on it.

The Bangkok-to-Chiang Mai flight takes fifty minutes.

He has never once wasted one.
