# Sahil Lavingia: The Failed Unicorn Who Found Something Better

In January 2019, Sahil Lavingia laid off 75% of his company.

He had raised $8 million in venture capital. He had 85 employees. He had been on Forbes 30 Under 30. He had a company that by every external measure was succeeding, and by every internal measure was dying.

The layoffs took a day. The lesson took years.

---

## The First Act

Sahil Lavingia was, by any reasonable accounting, a prodigy.

He built the first iPhone app for Pinterest at nineteen. He was the second employee at Pinterest before it was Pinterest. He left to start his own company, Gumroad, a platform that let creators sell digital products directly to their audiences.

He raised $1.1 million in a first round. Then $7 million more. He had top-tier investors, prestigious press, the full apparatus of Silicon Valley belief pointed at him.

He was twenty-two years old.

He hired. He expanded. He built features. He chased the metrics that venture capital demands: growth, growth, growth, and then more growth after that.

And then the growth stopped.

---

## The Dark Year

Gumroad was not failing in the way startups usually fail. People were using it. Creators were selling things on it. Revenue was real.

But the growth curve had flattened. Venture capital does not reward flatness. Flatness, in that world, is a kind of dying.

He tried to raise a new round. Investors passed. Thirty of them. He called it "a very humbling experience" in a Medium post that became required reading for every founder who had ever felt the gap between the story they told the world and the reality they lived inside it.

He laid off the team. He moved back into his parents' house. He had a company that was generating real revenue with real users and he had, in the logic of Silicon Valley, failed.

It was the best thing that ever happened to him.

---

## The Reframe

Alone with a product, no investors, no employees, no runway pressure, Sahil Lavingia did something unusual: he asked himself what he actually wanted.

Not what the market wanted. Not what investors wanted. What he wanted.

The answer surprised him.

He wanted to build software. He wanted to help creators sell things. He did not want to manage a team of eighty-five people. He did not want to run board meetings. He did not want the company to be a unicorn.

He wanted it to be sustainable.

He wrote a Medium post in 2019 titled "Reflecting on My Failure to Build a Billion-Dollar Company." It got 1.5 million readers. Not because it was a failure story — because it was an honest story, and honest stories are rare enough online that they travel.

---

## The Pivot to Sustainable

Sahil rebuilt Gumroad as a one-person company.

He automated what could be automated. He focused development on the things that actually moved the needle for creators. He stopped building features that were impressive to investors and started building features that were useful to sellers.

He made a radical decision: he would share the financials publicly. Revenue, churn, every number, posted for the world to see. Radical transparency as product. Radical transparency as trust.

In 2021, Gumroad processed $142 million in payments to creators. One person running it. He made enough to live well. He had time to paint — he had become a serious painter during the dark years — and write, and think.

The $8 million he'd raised and spent chasing billion-dollar metrics had delivered a company that nearly killed him. The company he rebuilt alone, without pressure, delivered everything he actually wanted.

---

## What He Learned

"Build a company you want to run, not a company investors want to fund."

This is the lesson Sahil Lavingia is most associated with now. It sounds obvious in retrospect. It is almost never obvious in the moment, when the pressure of outside expectations is so total that your own preferences feel like an indulgence you cannot afford.

He could afford them. He just didn't know it yet.

The layoffs in January 2019 were the worst day of his professional life. They were also the day he stopped building someone else's company and started building his own.

---

## The Last Thing

Sahil Lavingia still runs Gumroad.

He still posts the numbers publicly. He still paints. He hired a small team, eventually — not because he had to, but because he chose to.

The Forbes 30 Under 30 is framed somewhere he no longer looks at.

The painting he finished last month is hanging in his living room.

He knows which one matters more.
