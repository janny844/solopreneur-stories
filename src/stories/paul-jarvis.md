# Paul Jarvis: The Designer Who Argued That Growth Is a Choice

Paul Jarvis spent years as a web designer for large clients.

Mercedes-Benz. Microsoft. Warner Music. Yahoo. The kind of client list that designers build careers around and put at the top of their portfolios forever.

He walked away from all of it.

Not because the work was bad. Because the life around the work was bad — the meetings, the scale, the account managers, the sense that his time was being spent on things that would be replaced in eighteen months.

He moved to a small island off the coast of British Columbia and started working alone.

---

## Company of One

Paul Jarvis's central argument, eventually published as the book *Company of One*, is deceptively simple: growth is not automatically good.

Every business assumption points the other way. Growth is the metric. Growth is the goal. A company that isn't growing is dying.

His counterargument: growth introduces complexity, and complexity introduces cost — in management overhead, in coordination, in stress, in the erosion of the reasons you started. A one-person business that earns $200,000 and requires thirty hours a week is, for most people, a better outcome than a twenty-person business that earns $2 million and requires eighty.

The question is not "how do I grow" but "what is enough, and what does it cost to get there?"

Almost nobody asks the second question.

## The Products

Paul Jarvis did not write a book about small business and then have no business.

He built products: courses on freelancing and design, books, and — most substantially — Fathom Analytics, a privacy-focused alternative to Google Analytics, co-founded with Jack Ellis.

Fathom is the practical demonstration of the philosophy. It competes with a Google product. It has two founders and a tiny team. It does not raise money. It does not chase enterprise contracts requiring a sales organization.

It is profitable and growing and small on purpose.

---

## The Privacy Bet

Fathom Analytics succeeded on a positioning bet that looked risky in 2018 and looks obvious now.

Google Analytics was free, dominant, and universal. Competing with free is generally a losing proposition.

But Google Analytics was free because the product was the data, and the customer was Google. Website owners were increasingly uncomfortable with that — especially after GDPR made the legal exposure concrete.

Fathom charged money and collected no personal data. Simple dashboard, no cookie banner required, no user tracking.

It turned out a meaningful number of people would pay for software that did not surveil their visitors.

---

## Living the Argument

What distinguishes Paul Jarvis from most people who write about work-life philosophy is that he actually lives on the island.

He works limited hours. He does not scale. He turns down opportunities that would require hiring. He has said no to acquisition conversations, to expansion, to the standard next steps.

He has been doing this for over a decade, publicly, with real revenue.

The argument is not theoretical. It is a functioning demonstration that has now run long enough to be evidence rather than opinion.

---

## The Last Thing

Paul Jarvis had a client list that most designers would trade years for.

He left it to work alone on a small island, and then spent a decade arguing — with a book, with a business, with his actual daily life — that the thing everyone is chasing might not be worth catching.

The most radical sentence in *Company of One* is the simplest one:

"What if the goal is not more?"

Nobody had asked him that. So he asked it himself, and then he built a life around the answer.
