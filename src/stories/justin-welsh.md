# Justin Welsh: The Burned-Out VP Who Built a $5M One-Person Business

In October 2019, Justin Welsh had a panic attack in a WeWork bathroom in Los Angeles.

He was the SVP of Sales at a healthcare startup. He was responsible for a team of fifty people and a revenue number in the tens of millions. He was, by the metrics his industry used, successful.

He was also done.

He sat on the bathroom floor for twenty minutes, texted his wife that he wasn't okay, and drove home. He never went back to the office.

---

## Before the Burnout

Justin Welsh had spent fifteen years building other people's companies.

He was good at it — genuinely good, not just-good-enough good. He had a gift for sales, for building teams, for the particular blend of pressure and support that turns individual salespeople into systems that compound. He had climbed. He had earned. He had the LinkedIn profile that other people screenshot as an example of what to work toward.

He had also, somewhere in the grinding accumulation of seventy-hour weeks, entirely lost himself.

His identity had become his title. His worth had become his quota. When the quota was hit he felt briefly, hollowly good. When it wasn't, he felt like nothing.

The panic attack in the WeWork bathroom was the end of a story he had been telling himself for fifteen years. The story was: more is more. Work harder, earn more, achieve more, be more.

The bathroom floor was where that story ended.

---

## The Bet on Content

Justin Welsh made a specific bet in early 2020, while he was recovering and figuring out what came next: he would build an audience on LinkedIn.

Not for any particular reason at first. He just had things to say — about sales, about burnout, about the gap between what corporate success looks like and what it feels like. He thought maybe a few thousand people in sales might find it useful.

He started posting. Monday through Friday, one post per day. Short, specific, honest. Not inspirational content — reflective content. The difference between "here's how to be great" and "here's what I got wrong and what I actually learned from it."

The growth was slow at first. Then it wasn't.

LinkedIn's algorithm rewards consistency the way almost no other platform does. Six months of daily posting had built him 50,000 followers. A year in: 150,000.

At 300,000 followers, he launched his first product.

---

## The $150 Course

The course was called "The LinkedIn Operating System." It taught people to build LinkedIn audiences the way he had — systematically, sustainably, without burning out.

Price: $150.

He launched to his email list on a Tuesday. He made $100,000 in the first week.

He stared at the number for a long time.

This is the moment most people describe as the turning point. Justin Welsh describes it differently. "The money was validating," he says. "But the emails were the thing. Hundreds of people writing to say that the framework had changed how they thought about their careers. That was the thing."

The course became a business. The business became multiple courses. The courses became a newsletter, then a community, then a second newsletter. By 2023, Justin Welsh was generating over $5 million per year as a one-person business.

---

## The Architecture of One

Justin Welsh is precise about what makes the solo model work.

**Leverage over labor.** A course taught once to thousands earns more per hour than any consulting arrangement. Writing an email newsletter that goes to 200,000 people is the same effort as writing one that goes to 20. The internet makes your work infinitely replicable once it exists. The goal is to create things that exist.

**The flywheel.** Content builds audience. Audience buys products. Products generate revenue. Revenue buys time. Time creates content. Once the flywheel is spinning, it is very hard to stop.

**Saying no.** The most counterintuitive lesson from scaling a one-person business is that growth requires elimination. Every speaking engagement he doesn't take is a week he has to write. Every partnership he declines is a month he doesn't spend managing someone else's expectations. The discipline of the solo model is the discipline of refusal.

---

## What He Tells People Now

Justin Welsh is frequently asked how to replicate what he did.

His answer is always the same: pick one platform, post every day for a year, be specific about what you know, be honest about what you got wrong.

Most people don't do this because it sounds too simple. He understands. He spent fifteen years believing that more complicated meant more valuable.

The WeWork bathroom floor disagreed.

---

## The Last Thing

In October 2019, Justin Welsh sat on a bathroom floor in Los Angeles and texted his wife that he wasn't okay.

In October 2023, he was running a $5 million business from his home office, working four days a week, taking Fridays off to be present with his family.

He still posts on LinkedIn every day. Not because he has to.

Because it turns out that the thing that burned him out was not the work. It was the work in service of someone else's goals.

The same hours, directed toward his own, felt entirely different.
