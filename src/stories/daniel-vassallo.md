# Daniel Vassallo: The Amazon Engineer Who Quit For a Portfolio of Small Bets

Daniel Vassallo spent eight years at Amazon.

He was a senior engineer. He worked on AWS. He had stock vesting, a title, and a compensation package that most people would consider a destination rather than a waypoint.

In 2019, he quit with no plan.

Not with a startup idea. Not with a co-founder. Not with runway calculations and a business model. He quit because he had concluded that the trade he was making — his best years for a salary — was a worse deal than it looked.

---

## The Anti-Startup Position

Daniel Vassallo's most influential idea is a rejection of the standard startup narrative.

The standard narrative: find a big idea, commit everything, grind for years, exit big or fail.

His alternative: make many small bets. Build small things that generate small amounts of money. Don't optimize for the one big outcome. Optimize for having many independent sources of modest income, because the portfolio is more robust than the single bet.

He called it "small bets."

The insight underneath it is about risk, not ambition. A single startup is a concentrated position with a high probability of total loss. Twelve small products, each earning $1,000/month, produce the same income with dramatically less variance and no dependence on any single one succeeding.

---

## The Gumroad Products

Daniel Vassallo's first successful product was a $50 guide about using AWS — *Everyone Can Build a Twitter Bot*. Then a spreadsheet course. Then a book about his own quitting. Then a course about building an audience.

None of them were large. Together they generated real income.

He published his revenue publicly, which is how the community around him formed. The numbers were modest by startup standards and remarkable by "person who quit their job with no plan" standards.

Within eighteen months of leaving Amazon, he was earning more than his Amazon salary from products he owned entirely.

---

## Small Bets Community

The Small Bets community launched in 2021 as a one-time-payment membership: pay once, get lifetime access to a community of people making small bets.

The one-time payment was philosophically consistent with everything else he believed. Subscriptions create obligation — an ongoing promise to deliver value forever. A one-time payment ends the transaction cleanly.

It also happened to work extremely well as a business. Thousands of members joined. Revenue crossed seven figures.

The community became one of the more genuinely active places on the internet for people building small products, precisely because the incentive structure did not require constant re-selling.

---

## The Freedom Calculation

Daniel Vassallo talks about a specific calculation that most employees never run.

Your salary looks like security. But it is one customer — your employer — providing 100% of your income, with the ability to terminate the relationship at will, with no notice, for reasons entirely outside your control.

By any risk framework, that is an extraordinarily concentrated position.

Twelve products with a thousand customers each is diversified. A job is not.

"Employment is the riskiest way to make a living," he has argued. "It just doesn't feel that way because everyone is doing it."

---

## The Last Thing

Daniel Vassallo left a senior engineering position at Amazon with no plan and no safety net beyond savings.

He then spent years demonstrating, publicly and with numbers, that the safest path was not the one everyone assumed.

He never built a large company. He never wanted to.

He built enough small things that no single failure could hurt him — which is, when you look at it honestly, the actual definition of security.
