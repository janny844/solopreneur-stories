# Steph Smith: The Researcher Who Turned Curiosity Into a Career

Steph Smith was working a remote job at Toptal when she started noticing something.

The internet had made it possible to find almost anything — but almost nobody was systematically looking. The data was public. The trends were visible. The signals were sitting in plain sight, unread, because reading them required patience that most people didn't have.

She had the patience.

She started writing down what she found.

---

## Doing Content Right

Steph Smith's first significant self-published product was an ebook: *Doing Content Right*.

It was a comprehensive guide to building an audience through writing — not a thin listicle expanded into a PDF, but an actual book based on her own experience growing a blog from nothing while working full-time.

She launched it at $99, then $199 for a bundle.

Revenue in the first weeks: over $30,000.

The book worked because it was written by someone who had done the thing recently enough to remember what was hard. Most content marketing advice is written by people who built audiences in 2012 and are describing a channel that no longer works. Hers was current.

---

## Trends.co

Steph Smith joined The Hustle and became the lead on Trends — a paid research product that identified emerging business opportunities before they were obvious.

The job was, essentially, to be professionally curious. Find the industries that were quietly compounding. Find the tools that operators were adopting before analysts noticed. Find the demographic shifts that would matter in three years.

This was work she had already been doing for free.

Trends grew into a substantial paid community of founders and operators, and Steph Smith became one of its most recognized voices — the person who could look at a niche market and explain why it mattered.

---

## The Internet Pipes

Steph Smith's most-cited work is her writing about how the internet actually functions — the physical infrastructure, the undersea cables, the data centers, the routing.

"Internet Pipes" — her talk and accompanying resources about finding information online — became a cult resource among researchers, journalists, and founders. It is a guide to the tools and techniques for finding things most people assume are unfindable.

Satellite imagery. Public records. Shipping manifests. Job posting archives. Patent filings. Wayback Machine snapshots.

The premise: information asymmetry is a competitive advantage, and the internet has drastically lowered the cost of eliminating it — for anyone willing to learn where to look.

She taught people where to look.

---

## The Career Design

Steph Smith's career is unusual in that she has never quite been one thing.

Writer, researcher, podcast host, product lead, investor, speaker. She went from Toptal to The Hustle to a16z, and along the way built a personal body of work — books, essays, tools — that belonged to her regardless of her employer.

This is the modern version of a portfolio career, and it works because the personal body of work is real. Not personal branding as decoration on top of a job, but genuine independent output that would have value on its own.

Every role she has taken has been because someone read something she wrote.

---

## The Last Thing

Steph Smith noticed that the information was all sitting there, public and unread.

She read it. She wrote down what she found. She kept doing that for years across changing jobs and platforms.

The curiosity was not a strategy at first. It was just how she was.

It became a career because she wrote it down — and because writing it down is the difference between being someone who knows things and being someone people can find.
