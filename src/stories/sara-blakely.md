# Sara Blakely: The Fax Machine Saleswoman Who Cut the Feet Off Her Pantyhose

Sara Blakely was twenty-seven years old and selling fax machines door to door in Florida.

She had failed the LSAT twice. She had worked at Disney World as a chipmunk. She was now, on most days, being physically escorted out of office buildings by people who did not want to buy a fax machine.

She had $5,000 in savings.

One night, getting ready for a party, she cut the feet off a pair of control-top pantyhose so she could wear them under white pants with open-toed shoes.

They rolled up her legs all evening. But the look was right.

She thought: someone should fix this.

---

## The Two Years of No

Sara Blakely spent two years being told no.

She drove to North Carolina, the center of American hosiery manufacturing, and visited mills. Every mill turned her down. She had no fashion background, no manufacturing experience, no capital, no patent, no orders.

She was a fax machine saleswoman with an idea about pantyhose.

The mills were run by men. The product was worn by women. Not one of the decision-makers she pitched had ever worn the product they manufactured.

One mill owner eventually called her back. He had shown her prototype to his daughters. His daughters had told him it was a good idea.

That is the entire reason Spanx exists.

---

## Writing Her Own Patent

Sara Blakely wrote her own patent application.

Not because she was qualified — because a patent attorney quoted her $3,000 to $5,000, and she had $5,000 total and needed it for manufacturing.

She went to a Barnes & Noble, bought a book on patent law, and wrote the application herself. She hired a lawyer only for the final filing.

She also designed the packaging herself, chose red because every other hosiery package was beige or nude, and used a hand-drawn cartoon of three women because photographs of models would have cost money she didn't have.

Every constraint produced a decision that turned out to be right.

---

## Neiman Marcus

Sara Blakely called Neiman Marcus and asked for ten minutes with a buyer.

She got the meeting. Partway through the pitch, she could see it wasn't landing. So she asked the buyer to come with her to the bathroom.

In the bathroom, she changed into white pants — once without Spanx, once with — and showed her the difference.

Neiman Marcus placed an order for seven stores.

She then called everyone she knew in those seven cities and asked them to go buy Spanx, promising to reimburse them. She wanted the initial numbers to justify a larger order.

They did. It did.

---

## The Oprah Effect

In 2000, Oprah Winfrey named Spanx one of her Favorite Things.

Sara Blakely, who was still working her fax machine job during the day and building Spanx at night, had not yet quit. She was packing and shipping orders from her apartment.

After Oprah, she quit.

Revenue in the first year: $4 million. Second year: $10 million.

She had no investors, no advertising budget, and no formal business training. She had a product women wanted and a story that traveled.

---

## The Billion

In 2012, Forbes named Sara Blakely the youngest self-made female billionaire in the world.

She had never taken outside investment. She owned 100% of the company. When she eventually sold a majority stake to Blackstone in 2021, the deal valued Spanx at $1.2 billion.

She gave every employee two first-class plane tickets and $10,000 to travel.

---

## The Last Thing

Sara Blakely was selling fax machines door to door and getting thrown out of buildings.

She cut the feet off a pair of pantyhose because she wanted to wear white pants to a party.

Two years of rejection later, a mill owner's daughters told him it was a good idea.

Everything after that — the billion dollars, the Forbes cover, the Blackstone deal — followed from one woman refusing to accept that the reason nobody had fixed an obvious problem was that it couldn't be fixed.

Nobody had fixed it because nobody with the problem had been in the room.

She put herself in the room.
