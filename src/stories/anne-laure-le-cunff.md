# Anne-Laure Le Cunff: The Google Manager Who Chose Her Mind Over Her Career

Anne-Laure Le Cunff left Google on a Thursday.

She had a good title, a good salary, and a growing sense that she was spending her best hours building something she didn't believe in.

She had been reading neuroscience research in her spare time. Not casually — obsessively. She read papers. She took notes. She wrote things down not because anyone asked her to but because the act of writing made the thinking stick.

She decided to turn the writing into a publication. She had no subscribers, no editor, no monetization plan.

She called it Ness Labs.

---

## The Overlap

Ness Labs started as a personal experiment in learning in public.

Anne-Laure Le Cunff had been developing frameworks for herself — ways to take notes, ways to manage attention, ways to learn new things without burning out. She had a background in neuroscience (she was completing a PhD) and a background in tech (she had worked at Google on product teams). The intersection was unusual enough to be interesting.

She wrote about the neuroscience of habits, the psychology of creativity, the cognitive costs of context switching. She wrote for the version of herself that had been running on caffeine and willpower at Google, wishing someone would give her a user manual for her own brain.

The first few posts went nowhere. The next few went somewhere small. After six months of weekly writing, Ness Labs had 10,000 subscribers.

Not from one viral post. From consistent, specific, honest writing about a topic almost no one else was covering with the same combination of rigor and accessibility.

---

## The Membership Model

Anne-Laure Le Cunff launched Ness Labs as a membership: a paid community for "curious minds" who wanted the research, the writing, and the community of people thinking about the same things.

Price: £9/month.

This was not a conventional content business model. Most newsletters were free, monetized through sponsorships or course sales. A membership implied ongoing value — that the community itself was part of what you were buying.

She believed the community was part of it. She was right.

The Ness Labs community became a place where academics, therapists, designers, developers, and writers found each other around shared questions: how do we learn better? How do we work without burning out? How do we stay curious without being overwhelmed?

By 2022, Ness Labs had thousands of paid members and had become a significant part of the broader "mindful productivity" conversation online.

---

## The PhD Alongside

While running Ness Labs, Anne-Laure was completing a PhD in neuroscience at King's College London.

She has written about this decision — the decision to pursue both the academic credential and the public-facing business simultaneously — as a deliberate choice about identity. She did not want to be just a content creator. She wanted the research to be real, not just cited.

The PhD gave the content credibility. The content funded the lifestyle that made the PhD possible. Each supported the other.

"The goal was never to optimize for one metric," she has said. "The goal was to build a life with enough freedom to be curious."

---

## What She Built

Ness Labs is not one thing. It is a newsletter, a membership, a podcast, a set of courses, a community. Anne-Laure Le Cunff built each piece deliberately, testing what served the members and removing what didn't.

The through-line is always the question: what helps people think better, learn better, live better?

She has never chased virality. The most-shared pieces on Ness Labs are the most specific ones — the ones where she goes deep on a single study or a single framework and explains exactly how to apply it.

Specificity is generosity, in this context. The vague post helps no one. The specific post changes how someone works this week.

---

## The Last Thing

Anne-Laure Le Cunff left Google on a Thursday.

She started writing the following Monday.

The readers came, slowly, and then stayed. The community formed around a shared question that she had been asking herself.

She is still asking it. She has just found, along the way, that tens of thousands of people were asking it too — and that bringing those people together was itself the answer.
