# The Night Shift Nurse: He Built a Business Between Patients

The hospital was quiet at 3 AM.

He had finished his rounds. The charts were updated. The next scheduled check was in forty minutes. He opened his laptop in the break room and started writing code.

He had been doing this for eight months — building a small application in twenty and forty minute increments, in the gaps between patients, on night shifts that ran twelve hours.

He was not a professional developer. He had learned from YouTube tutorials and Stack Overflow answers, badly and slowly, in the same fragmented time.

---

## The Problem He Knew

His product came from his job.

Nurses on his floor were spending significant time on shift handover — the process of transferring patient information from the outgoing nurse to the incoming one. The hospital's official system was a mainframe interface designed in the nineties. Most nurses bypassed it entirely and used handwritten notes on folded paper, which they then transcribed twice.

It was slow. It was error-prone. It was, in a hospital, actually dangerous.

He built a small tool that did one thing: structured shift handover notes, on a phone, in a format nurses could fill in during the shift and hand off in ninety seconds.

He built it for himself and three colleagues.

---

## The Spread

The tool spread the way useful things spread inside institutions: sideways, quietly, without official approval.

A nurse used it. She told a friend on another floor. That friend told someone at a different hospital. Within a year, several hundred nurses across a dozen facilities were using a tool built by a night-shift nurse in break-room increments.

None of them were paying. He had not thought to charge.

A nurse manager eventually asked whether the hospital could license it for the whole department.

That was the moment it became a business.

---

## The Constraint That Helped

He built the entire first version in fragments of twenty to forty minutes.

He has said, since, that the constraint made the product better.

He could not architect elaborately, because he never had a long enough uninterrupted block to hold a complex design in his head. So he built the smallest possible thing that worked, then the next smallest thing, then the next.

The result was a product with no unnecessary features, because he had never had time to build unnecessary features.

Founders with unlimited time build bloated products. Founders with forty minutes build the thing that matters.

---

## Leaving the Floor

He did not quit nursing when the business started making money.

He went part-time for eighteen months, working three shifts a week while building, because the shifts were where the product feedback came from. Every night he used his own tool. Every night he noticed what was wrong with it.

Losing the connection to the floor would have meant losing the thing that made the product good.

He eventually stopped nursing when the business was earning several times his salary and he could no longer justify the hours.

He still says he misses the night shift.

---

## The Last Thing

The hospital was quiet at 3 AM and he opened a laptop in a break room.

He was not trying to build a company. He was trying to make his own job less annoying, forty minutes at a time, across eight months of night shifts.

The best products come from people who have the problem, not people who researched the problem.

He had the problem every night for years.

That was the entire advantage.
