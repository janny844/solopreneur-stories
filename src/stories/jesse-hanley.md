# Jesse Hanley: The Maker Who Built Bento From a Dozen Failures

Jesse Hanley had shipped a lot of things that didn't work.

Not spectacular failures — quiet ones. Products launched to indifference. Ideas that had seemed obviously good in the planning stage and turned out to have no buyers. Weeks and months of work absorbed by things that generated a handful of signups and then nothing.

This is the most common experience in indie software and the least documented, because nobody writes a retrospective about a product that made $40.

He kept shipping anyway.

---

## The Pattern Under the Failures

Looking back across the failed products, Jesse Hanley noticed something.

Every one of them had needed the same infrastructure: email capture, user tracking, behavioral triggers, automated sequences. Every time, he had cobbled it together from a marketing tool that was either too expensive, too limited, or too clearly built for a different kind of business.

The existing options were built for large marketing teams at established companies. They were priced for those teams, structured for those workflows, and full of features that a solo maker would never touch while missing the ones they needed daily.

He had experienced this a dozen times without recognizing it as a signal.

---

## Bento

Bento launched as a marketing automation platform built for the way software businesses actually work.

Not "email marketing" in the newsletter sense. Behavioral automation: track what users do inside your product, trigger messages based on that behavior, connect the data to the messaging without requiring an integration project.

The pitch was to a specific person: the technical founder running a SaaS business who needed real automation and did not want to pay enterprise marketing prices or hire someone to operate it.

Jesse Hanley knew that person precisely, because he had been that person a dozen times.

---

## Building for a Person You've Been

The design decisions in Bento reflect a founder who has felt the specific frustrations.

Developer-first APIs, because the intended user can write code. Transparent pricing, because the intended user has been burned by "contact sales." Automation logic that is powerful rather than simplified, because the intended user does not need training wheels.

None of these are obvious from market research. They come from having been the customer.

---

## The Long Middle

Bento did not explode. It grew.

Jesse Hanley has been candid about the years of steady, unglamorous building — support tickets, infrastructure work, incremental improvements, slow customer growth compounding month over month.

There was no Product Hunt moment that changed everything. There was a long sequence of months where the number went up slightly.

This is the actual texture of most successful bootstrapped software, and it is almost never the story that gets told, because "we grew 4% this month for forty consecutive months" makes a terrible headline and an excellent business.

---

## What the Failures Were For

The dozen failed products were not wasted.

They were the market research. Each one taught something about what customers actually pay for, what they say they want versus what they use, how much work a launch requires, how quickly indifference sets in.

More importantly, they produced the observation that became Bento — an observation that was only visible after building the same infrastructure a dozen times.

You cannot see a pattern from one instance.

---

## The Last Thing

Jesse Hanley shipped a dozen things that didn't work.

The thirteenth worked because the first twelve had shown him, through repetition, what was missing every single time.

The failures were not the price of success. They were the mechanism of it.

Nobody wants to hear that. It is true anyway.
