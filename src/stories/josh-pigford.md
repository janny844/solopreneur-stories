# Josh Pigford: The Founder Who Sold Baremetrics and Kept Building Anyway

Josh Pigford was running a business and had no idea whether it was working.

He was selling software. Stripe was collecting the money. But Stripe's dashboard told him about transactions, not about the business — no MRR, no churn rate, no lifetime value, no cohort analysis. The numbers he needed to make decisions did not exist anywhere.

He built a dashboard for himself over a weekend.

He mentioned it on Twitter.

Other founders asked if they could use it.

---

## Baremetrics

Baremetrics launched in 2013 as analytics for Stripe-based subscription businesses.

The pitch was one sentence: connect your Stripe account, see your real metrics.

It grew fast, because the problem was universal among a specific and growing group of people. Every SaaS founder using Stripe had the same blind spot. Josh Pigford had built the exact fix and priced it as a monthly subscription.

Within a few years, Baremetrics was doing over $100,000 in monthly recurring revenue.

---

## Open Startups

Josh Pigford made an unusual decision early: he made Baremetrics' own metrics public.

Anyone could see the company's MRR, churn, customer count, growth rate — live, in real time, at a public URL.

This was, at the time, close to unthinkable. Startups guard their metrics obsessively. Revealing churn is revealing weakness. Revealing MRR is telling competitors exactly how much room they have.

He did it anyway, and it became the single best marketing decision the company ever made.

The transparency generated coverage, credibility, and a movement — the "open startups" movement — that dozens of other companies joined. Baremetrics was not just a metrics tool. It was the company that had made metrics public.

Product-market fit and brand fit, aligned perfectly.

## The Hard Years

Baremetrics was not a smooth ride.

Josh Pigford has written honestly about the middle years — the period where growth flattened, where competitors appeared, where the work stopped being fun and became maintenance. He wrote about burnout. He wrote about anxiety. He wrote about wondering whether the company he had built was the company he wanted to run.

He also wrote about a failed acquisition attempt that fell apart late in the process, which is the kind of thing most founders never disclose.

The transparency extended to the difficult parts, which is why people trusted it.

---

## The Exit

In 2020, Josh Pigford sold Baremetrics.

Reported price: several million dollars, with the exact structure partially disclosed.

He had built a company from a weekend project to a multi-million-dollar exit over seven years, as a solo founder who eventually built a small team, with no venture capital.

He then did the thing that surprised people: he immediately started building again.

---

## Maybe

Josh Pigford's next company was Maybe — a personal finance product, attempting to solve a problem that has defeated many well-funded companies.

He built it, ran it, hit walls, and eventually open-sourced it when the business model didn't work the way he needed it to.

He has been public about that too.

Which is the through-line: seven years of Baremetrics, an exit, a new company, an honest post-mortem. Every stage documented, including the ones that didn't work.

---

## The Last Thing

Josh Pigford built a dashboard for himself over a weekend because he could not see his own numbers.

Seven years later he sold the company it became.

The thing that made it work was not the code — the code was replicable, and competitors did replicate it. What made it work was that he told the truth about the numbers, including his own, at a time when nobody else would.

Trust turned out to be the moat.

It usually is.
