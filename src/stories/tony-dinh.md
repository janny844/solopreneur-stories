# Tony Dinh: The Vietnamese Developer Who Built an Empire of Small Products

The first sale came at 3:14 AM.

Tony Dinh was asleep when it happened. He woke up to a Stripe notification on his phone — $29, from a stranger in Germany who had found his Mac app while searching for something else entirely.

He lay there in the dark in his apartment in Ho Chi Minh City and read the notification three times.

This was 2020. He had been a software engineer at a startup for three years. The salary was good. The work was fine. Everything was fine.

He quit four months later.

---

## The Calculation

Tony Dinh made a spreadsheet before he quit his job. Not a feelings spreadsheet, not a goals spreadsheet — a numbers spreadsheet.

If he made one product that earned $500/month, and then another, and then another, at some point the sum would exceed his salary. That was the whole calculation.

It was almost embarrassingly simple. But simple things have a way of being true that complicated things don't.

He had already made his first product — a Mac app called Tweetify that converted threads into images. It was making $200/month when he quit. Not enough to live on. Enough to prove the model worked.

He needed more products. He started building more products.

---

## The Portfolio Strategy

Most founders try to build one thing and make it big. Tony Dinh tried to build many things and make them sustainable.

By 2022, he had six products in market: a Twitter analytics tool, a Mac utility, a bookmark manager, a writing app, two smaller tools. Some made $50/month. Some made $2,000/month. None of them had made him rich.

Together, they paid his rent, his food, his occasional flight to a new country to work from for a month.

He called it the "portfolio of small bets" strategy before that phrase became a cliché. The logic was simple: if one product dies — and they do die, to algorithm changes, to competition, to changing markets — the others keep the lights on. Diversification as survival mechanism.

"I don't want to be dependent on one product," he wrote in a public post that went small-viral in the indie hacker community. "One product is fragile. Many products is a business."

---

## The Thing That Worked

In late 2021, Tony launched ScreenSnapAI — a screenshot enhancement tool for Mac.

He had built it for himself. He took a lot of screenshots for tutorials and documentation, and he was tired of the raw, ugly look of unformatted screenshots. He wanted something that would add a background, a shadow, a frame — automatically, without effort.

He built it over two weekends.

The launch was modest: a tweet, a Product Hunt post, an Indie Hackers thread. He went to sleep expecting nothing.

He woke up to $1,400 in sales.

Not $140. $1,400.

He refreshed Stripe three times to make sure it was real. It was real.

By the end of the month: $8,000. By the end of the year: $150,000.

From a tool he built for himself over two weekends.

---

## What He Understood That Others Didn't

Tony Dinh talks about three things when he explains the portfolio strategy.

**Distribution is product.** A better product that nobody finds beats a mediocre product with terrible distribution. He learned to launch in the right places — not just his Twitter audience, but Reddit communities, Hacker News, YouTube tutorials, Product Hunt timing. Each channel compounds differently. Some work immediately. Some pay off six months later when a YouTuber discovers your product and makes a video.

**Small is scalable.** Every feature he didn't build was a maintenance burden that didn't exist. His products are deliberately small — they do one thing, they do it well, and he resists the temptation to expand them into platforms. "A tool that does one thing is easy to explain, easy to maintain, and easy to sell."

**Your own pain is your best market research.** Every product he shipped that worked was something he needed himself. Every product that didn't work was something he built because he thought other people needed it. The gap between "I need this" and "I think others need this" is where most products die.

---

## The Revenue Now

In 2023, Tony Dinh's products collectively generated over $400,000 in revenue.

One person. Working from various cities across Southeast Asia. No employees, no investors, no office.

Six products, each doing its small part. A portfolio of bets that paid off because he kept placing them.

The Stripe notification that woke him at 3:14 AM in 2020 was a German stranger paying $29 for a Mac app.

By 2023, strangers were paying him $400,000 a year.

He still wakes up to Stripe notifications. He never gets tired of reading them.
