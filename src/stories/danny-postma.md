# Danny Postma: The Designer Who Accidentally Became a SaaS Founder

Danny Postma did not set out to build a SaaS.

He set out to make a wallpaper.

This is the part of the story he tells reluctantly, because it sounds too simple — almost embarrassingly simple — for something that would eventually generate $40,000 in a single month.

But there it is.

---

## The Tool He Built For Himself

In 2021, Danny Postma was a designer. Not a startup founder, not an entrepreneur with a five-year plan — a designer who took on client work and occasionally launched small projects that mostly went nowhere.

He needed headshots for a website. Real headshot photography was expensive — $200 at minimum, usually more, requiring a photographer and a location and the kind of availability that freelancers rarely have.

He wondered if AI could do it.

It couldn't. Not yet. Not well.

But he thought about it. He kept thinking about it. Over the following months, he watched the image generation models get better. He watched Stable Diffusion release. He watched the gap between "AI-generated image" and "usable professional photo" close, slowly, then faster.

In October 2022, he built a tool.

He called it Headshot Pro.

He wasn't sure it would work. He built it anyway.

---

## The Launch Nobody Saw Coming

He posted it on Twitter on a Tuesday.

No fanfare. No Product Hunt strategy. No email list warming campaign. He had maybe 3,000 Twitter followers — a modest audience, the kind that gets you a few hundred impressions on a normal day.

The tweet said: "I made a thing. Upload 10 photos, get 100 AI headshots back. It actually works."

He attached a screenshot.

Within four hours, the tweet had 400 retweets.

Danny Postma does not describe himself as a marketing person. He describes himself as someone who accidentally discovered that the most effective marketing is just showing something that genuinely surprises people.

The headshots surprised people.

Not because they were perfect — they weren't, not at launch. But because they were close enough to be useful, and close enough to be interesting, and interesting things on the internet travel fast.

By the end of that first day, he had made $4,300.

He had not told a single person he was launching. He had not sent a single email. He had posted one tweet and gone to make coffee.

---

## What He Did Next

Danny Postma made a decision that, in retrospect, looks obvious but at the time felt risky: he did not change anything.

He did not add features. He did not pivot. He did not raise his prices immediately or try to push the product into new markets.

He fixed what broke.

Because things broke. The queue backed up when too many users uploaded photos at once. The model occasionally returned something unusable — a professional headshot with six fingers, or a background that was technically correct but somehow slightly wrong.

He fixed the queue. He improved the model. He wrote better prompts.

Each fix made the product a little better. Each improvement generated a little more word of mouth. By December 2022 — two months after launch — he had made $200,000 in revenue.

One person. A designer who had never built a SaaS. No co-founder, no investor, no employee.

Just a product that worked, getting incrementally less broken over time.

---

## The Imitation

By January 2023, there were eleven products that looked exactly like Headshot Pro.

This is what happens when something works on the internet. The copycats arrive before the confetti does.

Danny Postma did not panic. He did something more interesting: he watched.

He watched the copycats compete on price. He watched them rush features he hadn't built. He watched them run ads he hadn't run. And he noticed something.

His customers were not leaving.

Not because his product was better in every technical dimension — it wasn't, by then. But because trust compounds. The people who had used Headshot Pro in October and told their colleagues about it in November did not care about the cheaper alternatives in January. They had a recommendation already. Recommendations don't refresh on a monthly basis.

"You cannot copy a head start," he wrote in a newsletter.

He was right.

---

## The Designer's Edge

Here is the thing about Danny Postma that the story usually forgets: the reason Headshot Pro worked was not the AI. Other people had access to the same AI models.

The reason it worked was the design.

The interface was clean in a way that made the product feel trustworthy before you'd used it. The upload flow was simple in a way that reduced friction at the exact moment users were most likely to abandon. The output presentation was polished in a way that made good results look great and mediocre results look passable.

Design is not decoration. It is the difference between a product people understand and a product people don't. Between a feature people discover and a feature people miss. Between revenue and refund requests.

He had spent ten years learning how to make things clear.

The AI did the impressive work. The design did the conversion.

Both were required. Only one of them was available to everyone.

---

## The Accidental Blueprint

Danny Postma did not set out to teach anyone how to build a SaaS.

But after Headshot Pro, he started writing about it — the launch, the numbers, the decisions. People read it. More people asked questions. He started a newsletter.

He now makes revenue from Headshot Pro, from a second SaaS product, and from the newsletter. Three income streams. One person. A designer who learned, by accident, that the skills he already had were worth more than he knew.

"The best business to start," he has written, "is the one where you're already an expert and nobody has made it easy yet."

He was a designer. Headshots were not easy yet.

Now they are.

He made them that way.

---

## The Number

$40,000 in a single month. Multiple months. From a tool he built because he needed a profile photo.

Some stories have a moment of genius at the center. A breakthrough idea, a clever insight, a calculated risk that paid off.

Danny Postma's story has a wallpaper.

And a problem he had himself.

And the willingness to build the answer even when he wasn't sure it would work.

That's the whole thing. That's all of it.
