# Corey Haines: The Marketer Who Built the Best Swipe File on the Internet

Corey Haines kept a folder.

Not a metaphorical folder — an actual folder on his desktop labeled "SWIPE" where he dragged screenshots of ads, emails, landing pages, and copy that made him stop scrolling.

By 2020, the folder had thousands of files. He had been collecting them for years — since his first marketing job, through his time as Head of Growth at Baremetrics, through freelance projects and consulting gigs and every other configuration of the marketing work he had done.

The folder was his education. It was also, he eventually realized, a product.

---

## The Swipe File Concept

A swipe file is a marketer's practice of collecting examples of great copy and creative work — things worth learning from, things worth imitating, things that work.

The concept is old. Ad copywriters in the 1950s kept physical folders. David Ogilvy had one. Gary Halbert had one. Every serious student of persuasion has some version of one.

What Corey Haines had that they didn't: a way to share it with thousands of people simultaneously.

He launched Swipe Files in 2020 as a membership — access to a searchable, categorized collection of marketing examples: ads, emails, landing pages, onboarding flows, subject lines, popups, pricing pages.

Every example was real. Every example was annotated with notes about what made it work.

---

## The Niche Within the Niche

Swipe Files was not the only marketing example resource on the internet. But it was the most focused on SaaS and software companies — the specific subset of marketing that Corey Haines had spent his career in.

This specificity was a strength.

A marketer at a consumer packaged goods company needed different examples than a marketer at a B2B SaaS startup. The swipe files that existed were too broad to be maximally useful to either.

Corey Haines built for the one he knew: the software marketer trying to improve conversion rates, onboarding flows, email sequences.

The members who joined were not casual browsers. They were practitioners who needed specific examples for specific problems. Their willingness to pay — $25/month for access — reflected the professional value, not the consumer entertainment value.

---

## The Paradox of Curated Content

Corey Haines built something that sounds easy and is genuinely hard: a curated collection.

Curation looks easy because the product is screenshots and annotations, not code or manufactured goods. But curation at quality is the exercise of taste — the ability to look at ten thousand examples and identify the thirty that teach something important, presented in a way that transfers the lesson.

That skill is rare and valuable and impossible to automate convincingly.

He kept the quality high by only adding examples he would personally use as a reference. If he wouldn't pull it out in a planning meeting as a model, it didn't go into the collection.

This constraint kept Swipe Files useful over time, as opposed to becoming another overwhelming archive where everything is technically available but nothing is findable.

---

## Beyond Swipe Files

Corey Haines has built other products since Swipe Files — a course, consulting offers, other tools. But Swipe Files remains the anchor: the recurring revenue, the trusted resource, the community.

He has said that the lesson of Swipe Files is about the power of specificity and the value of doing one thing well over a long time.

"There are thousands of marketing newsletters," he has written. "There is one Swipe Files. Being the one requires choosing to be nothing else."

---

## The Last Thing

The folder on Corey Haines's desktop was his education and his product, simultaneously.

He had collected it for himself. He shared it because other people needed it. They paid for it because it saved them hours of research every week.

The gap between "this is useful to me" and "this is a business" turned out to be smaller than he expected.

It usually is.
