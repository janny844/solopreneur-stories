Marc Lou: The Indie Hacker Who Sold $1M in Templates

At 11:47 PM on a Wednesday, Marc Lou refreshed his Stripe dashboard for the fourth time that hour.

$0.

He closed the laptop.

This was the part nobody posted about on Twitter. Not the revenue charts, not the follower counts — just a man in a small Paris apartment staring at a number that hadn’t moved in three days.

He had quit his job four months earlier. He had $6,000 in savings. He had a product he believed in. And he had, by every measurable metric, absolutely nothing to show for it.

Before the Money

Marc Lou was not a prodigy. He grew up in Lyon, studied engineering, took a job at a startup building internal tools for enterprises. The work was fine. The salary was fine. Everything about it was fine — which was, he would later say, the problem.

“Fine is the enemy of alive,” he wrote in a tweet years later. At the time, he couldn’t have articulated it that cleanly. He just felt the ceiling.

He started building things at night. A small Chrome extension. A JavaScript library nobody used. A productivity app that got fourteen downloads, twelve of which were probably him testing the install flow.

None of it worked. All of it mattered.

Because the thing about building in the dark is that you learn something even when nothing ships. You learn how to debug at midnight when your brain is tired. You learn how to ship ugly code that works over beautiful code that doesn’t. You learn that you can keep going after a failure, because what else are you going to do?

He built for two years before he built the thing that changed everything.

The Product Nobody Asked For

ShipFast started as a README file.

Marc had built a SaaS product — a small tool for indie hackers — and the process of setting it up had taken him three weeks of boilerplate: auth, payments, email, landing pages, database connections. Three weeks of infrastructure before he could write a single line of his actual product.

“I am never doing this again,” he thought.

So he packaged everything he’d built into a Next.js boilerplate and put it on Gumroad for $99.

He launched on a Tuesday. He told no one. He had no email list, no Twitter following worth mentioning, no Product Hunt campaign prepared. He just put it up and went to sleep.

He woke up to $297.

Three sales. Three strangers had found the thing, read the description, decided it was worth $99, and bought it. In his sleep.

He stared at the number for a long time.

The Pivot That Wasn’t

Here is what most people misunderstand about Marc Lou’s story: he did not suddenly become a marketing genius. He did not go viral. He did not crack some algorithm.

He just kept going.

He updated the boilerplate. He added features people asked for in the reviews. He raised the price to $149, then $199. Each time he raised the price, he braced for the sales to drop. Each time, they didn’t.

He started a Twitter account and posted about what he was building. Not threads with “🧵” at the top. Just honest, specific posts about what worked, what didn’t, how much he made that week.

The following grew because the content was real. People can tell. There is a texture to genuine struggle that manufactured content doesn’t have — a specificity, an awkwardness, a detail that no one would invent.

“Made $3,200 in February. Spent $180 on coffee. Net: still alive.”

That kind of thing.

The Number

Twelve months after launching ShipFast, Marc Lou had made $347,000.

Eighteen months in: $612,000.

Two years in: $1,000,000.

One person. No employees. No investors. No office. A laptop, a Gumroad page, and a boilerplate that saved developers three weeks of setup time.

The product had not fundamentally changed. The market had not expanded dramatically. He had not run a single paid ad.

What had changed was compound trust. Every honest tweet, every product update, every moment where he admitted something wasn’t working and then showed what he did about it — all of it stacked. The internet rewards consistency over time in a way it rarely rewards single moments of brilliance.

What He Learned (So You Don’t Have To)

Marc Lou talks about three things when people ask him how he did it.

The first is specificity. Generic products die. ShipFast survived because it solved one exact problem — the three weeks of setup — and solved it completely. It did not try to be a platform or a suite or an ecosystem. It tried to save you three weeks of your life. That’s it.

The second is price. He raised the price four times. Each time he was afraid. Each time the sales increased. The people who will pay $199 for something that saves them time are not the same people who balk at $99. The people willing to pay $199 are better customers — they value their time, they implement quickly, they don’t email you asking you to build the product for them.

The third is showing the process. Not the results — the process. The uncertain weeks. The features that flopped. The morning he almost shut it down. “People don’t want your success,” he says. “They want proof that someone like them succeeded. Show them you were someone like them.”

The Last Thing

Marc Lou still refreshes his Stripe dashboard.

He probably always will.

But the number is different now. And more importantly, so is what he feels when he sees it. Not relief. Not validation.

Just the quiet satisfaction of a man who built something real, alone, in a Paris apartment, at 11:47 PM on a Wednesday.

The rest is details.
