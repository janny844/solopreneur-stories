Marc Lou: The Indie Hacker Who Sold $1M in Templates

At 11:47 PM on a Wednesday, Marc Lou refreshed his Stripe dashboard for the fourth time that hour.

$0.

He closed the laptop.

This was the part nobody posted about on Twitter. Not the revenue charts, not the follower counts — just a man in a small Paris apartment staring at a number that hadn’t moved in three days.

He had quit his job four months earlier. He had $6,000 in savings. He had a product he believed in. And he had, by every measurable metric, absolutely nothing to show for it.

Before the Money

Marc Lou was not a prodigy. He grew up in Lyon, studied engineering, took a job at a startup building internal tools for enterprises. The work was fine. The salary was fine. Everything about it was fine — which was, he would later say, the problem.

“Fine is the enemy of alive,” he wrote in a tweet years later. At the time, he couldn’t have articulated it that cleanly. He just felt the ceiling.

He started building things at night. A small Chrome extension. A JavaScript library nobody used. A productivity app that got fourteen downloads, twelve of which were probably him testing the install flow.

None of it worked. All of it mattered.

Because the thing about building in the dark is that you learn something even when nothing ships. You learn how to debug at midnight when your brain is tired. You learn how to ship ugly code that works over beautiful code that doesn’t. You learn that you can keep going after a failure, because what else are you going to do?

He built for two years before he built the thing that changed everything.

The Product Nobody Asked For

ShipFast started as a README file.

Marc had built a SaaS product — a small tool for indie hackers — and the process of setting it up had taken him three weeks of boilerplate: auth, payments, email, landing pages, database connections. Three weeks of infrastructure before he could write a single line of his actual product.

“I am never doing this again,” he thought.

So he packaged everything he’d built into a Next.js boilerplate and put it on Gumroad for $99.

He launched on a Tuesday. He told no one. He had no email list, no Twitter following worth mentioning, no Product Hunt campaign prepared. He just put it up and went to sleep.

He woke up to $297.

Three sales. Three strangers had found the thing, read the description, decided it was worth $99, and bought it. In his sleep.

He stared at the number for a long time.

The Pivot That Wasn’t

Here is what most people misunderstand about Marc Lou’s story: he did not suddenly become a marketing genius. He did not go viral. He did not crack some algorithm.

He just kept going.

He updated the boilerplate. He added features people asked for in the reviews. He raised the price to $149, then $199. Each time he raised the price, he braced for the sales to drop. Each time, they didn’t.

He started a Twitter account and posted about what he was building. Not threads with “🧵” at the top. Just honest, specific posts about what worked, what didn’t, how much he made that week.

The following grew because the content was real. People can tell. There is a texture to genuine struggle that manufactured content doesn’t have — a specificity, an awkwardness, a detail that no one would invent.

“Made $3,200 in February. Spent $180 on coffee. Net: still alive.”

That kind of thing.

The Number

Twelve months after launching ShipFast, Marc Lou had made $347,000.

Eighteen months in: $612,000.

Two years in: $1,000,000.

One person. No employees. No investors. No office. A laptop, a Gumroad page, and a boilerplate that saved developers three weeks of setup time.

The product had not fundamentally changed. The market had not expanded dramatically. He had not run a single paid ad.

What had changed was compound trust. Every honest tweet, every product update, every moment where he admitted something wasn’t working and then showed what he did about it — all of it stacked. The internet rewards consistency over time in a way it rarely rewards single moments of brilliance.

What He Learned (So You Don’t Have To)

Marc Lou talks about three things when people ask him how he did it.

The first is specificity. Generic products die. ShipFast survived because it solved one exact problem — the three weeks of setup — and solved it completely. It did not try to be a platform or a suite or an ecosystem. It tried to save you three weeks of your life. That’s it.

The second is price. He raised the price four times. Each time he was afraid. Each time the sales increased. The people who will pay $199 for something that saves them time are not the same people who balk at $99. The people willing to pay $199 are better customers — they value their time, they implement quickly, they don’t email you asking you to build the product for them.

The third is showing the process. Not the results — the process. The uncertain weeks. The features that flopped. The morning he almost shut it down. “People don’t want your success,” he says. “They want proof that someone like them succeeded. Show them you were someone like them.”

The Last Thing

Marc Lou still refreshes his Stripe dashboard.

He probably always will.

But the number is different now. And more importantly, so is what he feels when he sees it. Not relief. Not validation.

Just the quiet satisfaction of a man who built something real, alone, in a Paris apartment, at 11:47 PM on a Wednesday.

The rest is details.

Pieter Levels: The Nomad Who Built 12 Startups in 12 Months

The flight from Bangkok to Chiang Mai takes fifty minutes.

Pieter Levels spent that flight writing code.

Not planning code. Not sketching on a napkin. Writing it — in a middle seat, on a 13-inch MacBook Air, with the tray table down and his elbows tucked against his sides because the man next to him was asleep and taking half the armrest.

This was 2014. He had been traveling for four months. He had launched two startups. Both had failed. He had $4,400 left in his account, no permanent address, and a public promise on his website that he intended to keep: twelve startups in twelve months.

He had ten left.

The Bet He Made With Himself

Pieter Levels is Dutch. He grew up in the Netherlands, studied at a university, played in a band, started a YouTube channel that reached three million subscribers before he shut it down because it felt like a job.

The pattern was already there, if you knew to look for it: he would build something, it would work, and then he would leave.

Not because things got hard. Because things got normal.

In 2013, he sold everything he owned — the furniture, the guitar, most of his clothes — and bought a one-way ticket to Asia. He was twenty-six. His plan was to travel indefinitely and fund it through online work.

The twelve startups idea came later, from frustration. He had too many ideas and not enough urgency. The public commitment was a forcing function — a way of saying: if you’re going to build these things, build them now, because you’ve told the entire internet you would.

The internet held him to it.

The Product That Worked

Startup number four was called Nomad List.

It started as a spreadsheet. A simple Google Sheets document where Pieter logged the cities he’d been to: cost of living, internet speed, weather, safety score. He made it public because sharing felt natural.

Within a week, 10,000 people had viewed it.

He turned it into a website in a weekend. No landing page, no investor pitch, no user research sessions. Just a database of cities and a simple interface to sort them. He launched it on Hacker News with a two-sentence post.

It got to the front page.

The traffic crashed his $5/month server three times that day. He upgraded the server between each crash. By the end of the week, Nomad List had 800,000 pageviews and Pieter had made $0 from it.

He added a $100/year membership the following Monday.

He made $1,000 in the first hour.

The Architecture of One Person

Here is what Pieter Levels understood before most people did: the internet makes it possible for one person to serve a million people, but only if you build the product to require zero people.

Every feature he didn’t build was a support ticket that would never arrive. Every decision he automated was a morning he didn’t spend in his inbox. He built Nomad List to run without him not because he was lazy, but because he was one person and there was a finite amount of him to go around.

He did not hire a team. He did not raise funding. Investors emailed. He ignored them.

“If I take investment, I have to do what the investor wants,” he said in an interview. “I don’t want to do what investors want. I want to do what I want.”

What he wanted, specifically, was to work four hours a day, travel wherever he liked, and build products that solved real problems for real people.

He made $2.7 million in revenue in 2023. Solo. From a beach in Thailand.

The Twelve Months

He finished the challenge.

Twelve startups. Not twelve successes — twelve launches. Some made money. Most didn’t. One, Remote OK, grew alongside Nomad List into a job board that served 100,000 monthly users.

The point was never to succeed twelve times. The point was to ship twelve times — to build the muscle memory of launching, of putting things in front of people, of seeing what happened when you stopped perfecting and started publishing.

“Every time I ship something and it doesn’t work,” he said, “I learn something worth more than if it had worked.”

He would know.

What He Carried Forward

The question people always ask Pieter Levels is: how do you stay motivated without a team, without an office, without the structure of a normal company?

He gives the same answer every time.

“I’m not motivated,” he says. “I’m curious.”

Motivation is emotional — it rises and falls with mood, sleep, weather. Curiosity is structural. It pulls you toward problems because you genuinely want to know what happens when you try to solve them.

The fifty-minute flight from Bangkok to Chiang Mai is long enough to build a feature if you already know what you’re building. The trick is always knowing what you’re building next.

He has never not known.

The Number

Pieter Levels’s publicly disclosed revenue is approximately $3 million per year.

One person. No office. No team. A laptop that lives in a backpack, a collection of small products that compound slowly into something large, and a calendar with no commute on it.

The Bangkok-to-Chiang Mai flight takes fifty minutes.

He has never once wasted one.

Danny Postma: The Designer Who Accidentally Became a SaaS Founder

Danny Postma did not set out to build a SaaS.

He set out to make a wallpaper.

This is the part of the story he tells reluctantly, because it sounds too simple — almost embarrassingly simple — for something that would eventually generate $40,000 in a single month.

But there it is.

The Tool He Built For Himself

In 2021, Danny Postma was a designer. Not a startup founder, not an entrepreneur with a five-year plan — a designer who took on client work and occasionally launched small projects that mostly went nowhere.

He needed headshots for a website. Real headshot photography was expensive — $200 at minimum, usually more, requiring a photographer and a location and the kind of availability that freelancers rarely have.

He wondered if AI could do it.

It couldn’t. Not yet. Not well.

But he thought about it. He kept thinking about it. Over the following months, he watched the image generation models get better. He watched Stable Diffusion release. He watched the gap between “AI-generated image” and “usable professional photo” close, slowly, then faster.

In October 2022, he built a tool.

He called it Headshot Pro.

He wasn’t sure it would work. He built it anyway.

The Launch Nobody Saw Coming

He posted it on Twitter on a Tuesday.

No fanfare. No Product Hunt strategy. No email list warming campaign. He had maybe 3,000 Twitter followers — a modest audience, the kind that gets you a few hundred impressions on a normal day.

The tweet said: “I made a thing. Upload 10 photos, get 100 AI headshots back. It actually works.”

He attached a screenshot.

Within four hours, the tweet had 400 retweets.

Danny Postma does not describe himself as a marketing person. He describes himself as someone who accidentally discovered that the most effective marketing is just showing something that genuinely surprises people.

The headshots surprised people.

Not because they were perfect — they weren’t, not at launch. But because they were close enough to be useful, and close enough to be interesting, and interesting things on the internet travel fast.

By the end of that first day, he had made $4,300.

He had not told a single person he was launching. He had not sent a single email. He had posted one tweet and gone to make coffee.

What He Did Next

Danny Postma made a decision that, in retrospect, looks obvious but at the time felt risky: he did not change anything.

He did not add features. He did not pivot. He did not raise his prices immediately or try to push the product into new markets.

He fixed what broke.

Because things broke. The queue backed up when too many users uploaded photos at once. The model occasionally returned something unusable — a professional headshot with six fingers, or a background that was technically correct but somehow slightly wrong.

He fixed the queue. He improved the model. He wrote better prompts.

Each fix made the product a little better. Each improvement generated a little more word of mouth. By December 2022 — two months after launch — he had made $200,000 in revenue.

One person. A designer who had never built a SaaS. No co-founder, no investor, no employee.

Just a product that worked, getting incrementally less broken over time.

The Imitation

By January 2023, there were eleven products that looked exactly like Headshot Pro.

This is what happens when something works on the internet. The copycats arrive before the confetti does.

Danny Postma did not panic. He did something more interesting: he watched.

He watched the copycats compete on price. He watched them rush features he hadn’t built. He watched them run ads he hadn’t run. And he noticed something.

His customers were not leaving.

Not because his product was better in every technical dimension — it wasn’t, by then. But because trust compounds. The people who had used Headshot Pro in October and told their colleagues about it in November did not care about the cheaper alternatives in January. They had a recommendation already. Recommendations don’t refresh on a monthly basis.

“You cannot copy a head start,” he wrote in a newsletter.

He was right.

The Designer’s Edge

Here is the thing about Danny Postma that the story usually forgets: the reason Headshot Pro worked was not the AI. Other people had access to the same AI models.

The reason it worked was the design.

The interface was clean in a way that made the product feel trustworthy before you’d used it. The upload flow was simple in a way that reduced friction at the exact moment users were most likely to abandon. The output presentation was polished in a way that made good results look great and mediocre results look passable.

Design is not decoration. It is the difference between a product people understand and a product people don’t. Between a feature people discover and a feature people miss. Between revenue and refund requests.

He had spent ten years learning how to make things clear.

The AI did the impressive work. The design did the conversion.

Both were required. Only one of them was available to everyone.

The Accidental Blueprint

Danny Postma did not set out to teach anyone how to build a SaaS.

But after Headshot Pro, he started writing about it — the launch, the numbers, the decisions. People read it. More people asked questions. He started a newsletter.

He now makes revenue from Headshot Pro, from a second SaaS product, and from the newsletter. Three income streams. One person. A designer who learned, by accident, that the skills he already had were worth more than he knew.

“The best business to start,” he has written, “is the one where you’re already an expert and nobody has made it easy yet.”

He was a designer. Headshots were not easy yet.

Now they are.

He made them that way.

The Number

$40,000 in a single month. Multiple months. From a tool he built because he needed a profile photo.

Some stories have a moment of genius at the center. A breakthrough idea, a clever insight, a calculated risk that paid off.

Danny Postma’s story has a wallpaper.

And a problem he had himself.

And the willingness to build the answer even when he wasn’t sure it would work.

That’s the whole thing. That’s all of it.

Tony Dinh: The Vietnamese Developer Who Built an Empire of Small Products

The first sale came at 3:14 AM.

Tony Dinh was asleep when it happened. He woke up to a Stripe notification on his phone — $29, from a stranger in Germany who had found his Mac app while searching for something else entirely.

He lay there in the dark in his apartment in Ho Chi Minh City and read the notification three times.

This was 2020. He had been a software engineer at a startup for three years. The salary was good. The work was fine. Everything was fine.

He quit four months later.

The Calculation

Tony Dinh made a spreadsheet before he quit his job. Not a feelings spreadsheet, not a goals spreadsheet — a numbers spreadsheet.

If he made one product that earned $500/month, and then another, and then another, at some point the sum would exceed his salary. That was the whole calculation.

It was almost embarrassingly simple. But simple things have a way of being true that complicated things don’t.

He had already made his first product — a Mac app called Tweetify that converted threads into images. It was making $200/month when he quit. Not enough to live on. Enough to prove the model worked.

He needed more products. He started building more products.

The Portfolio Strategy

Most founders try to build one thing and make it big. Tony Dinh tried to build many things and make them sustainable.

By 2022, he had six products in market: a Twitter analytics tool, a Mac utility, a bookmark manager, a writing app, two smaller tools. Some made $50/month. Some made $2,000/month. None of them had made him rich.

Together, they paid his rent, his food, his occasional flight to a new country to work from for a month.

He called it the “portfolio of small bets” strategy before that phrase became a cliché. The logic was simple: if one product dies — and they do die, to algorithm changes, to competition, to changing markets — the others keep the lights on. Diversification as survival mechanism.

“I don’t want to be dependent on one product,” he wrote in a public post that went small-viral in the indie hacker community. “One product is fragile. Many products is a business.”

The Thing That Worked

In late 2021, Tony launched ScreenSnapAI — a screenshot enhancement tool for Mac.

He had built it for himself. He took a lot of screenshots for tutorials and documentation, and he was tired of the raw, ugly look of unformatted screenshots. He wanted something that would add a background, a shadow, a frame — automatically, without effort.

He built it over two weekends.

The launch was modest: a tweet, a Product Hunt post, an Indie Hackers thread. He went to sleep expecting nothing.

He woke up to $1,400 in sales.

Not $140. $1,400.

He refreshed Stripe three times to make sure it was real. It was real.

By the end of the month: $8,000. By the end of the year: $150,000.

From a tool he built for himself over two weekends.

What He Understood That Others Didn’t

Tony Dinh talks about three things when he explains the portfolio strategy.

Distribution is product. A better product that nobody finds beats a mediocre product with terrible distribution. He learned to launch in the right places — not just his Twitter audience, but Reddit communities, Hacker News, YouTube tutorials, Product Hunt timing. Each channel compounds differently. Some work immediately. Some pay off six months later when a YouTuber discovers your product and makes a video.

Small is scalable. Every feature he didn’t build was a maintenance burden that didn’t exist. His products are deliberately small — they do one thing, they do it well, and he resists the temptation to expand them into platforms. “A tool that does one thing is easy to explain, easy to maintain, and easy to sell.”

Your own pain is your best market research. Every product he shipped that worked was something he needed himself. Every product that didn’t work was something he built because he thought other people needed it. The gap between “I need this” and “I think others need this” is where most products die.

The Revenue Now

In 2023, Tony Dinh’s products collectively generated over $400,000 in revenue.

One person. Working from various cities across Southeast Asia. No employees, no investors, no office.

Six products, each doing its small part. A portfolio of bets that paid off because he kept placing them.

The Stripe notification that woke him at 3:14 AM in 2020 was a German stranger paying $29 for a Mac app.

By 2023, strangers were paying him $400,000 a year.

He still wakes up to Stripe notifications. He never gets tired of reading them.

Sahil Lavingia: The Failed Unicorn Who Found Something Better

In January 2019, Sahil Lavingia laid off 75% of his company.

He had raised $8 million in venture capital. He had 85 employees. He had been on Forbes 30 Under 30. He had a company that by every external measure was succeeding, and by every internal measure was dying.

The layoffs took a day. The lesson took years.

The First Act

Sahil Lavingia was, by any reasonable accounting, a prodigy.

He built the first iPhone app for Pinterest at nineteen. He was the second employee at Pinterest before it was Pinterest. He left to start his own company, Gumroad, a platform that let creators sell digital products directly to their audiences.

He raised $1.1 million in a first round. Then $7 million more. He had top-tier investors, prestigious press, the full apparatus of Silicon Valley belief pointed at him.

He was twenty-two years old.

He hired. He expanded. He built features. He chased the metrics that venture capital demands: growth, growth, growth, and then more growth after that.

And then the growth stopped.

The Dark Year

Gumroad was not failing in the way startups usually fail. People were using it. Creators were selling things on it. Revenue was real.

But the growth curve had flattened. Venture capital does not reward flatness. Flatness, in that world, is a kind of dying.

He tried to raise a new round. Investors passed. Thirty of them. He called it “a very humbling experience” in a Medium post that became required reading for every founder who had ever felt the gap between the story they told the world and the reality they lived inside it.

He laid off the team. He moved back into his parents’ house. He had a company that was generating real revenue with real users and he had, in the logic of Silicon Valley, failed.

It was the best thing that ever happened to him.

The Reframe

Alone with a product, no investors, no employees, no runway pressure, Sahil Lavingia did something unusual: he asked himself what he actually wanted.

Not what the market wanted. Not what investors wanted. What he wanted.

The answer surprised him.

He wanted to build software. He wanted to help creators sell things. He did not want to manage a team of eighty-five people. He did not want to run board meetings. He did not want the company to be a unicorn.

He wanted it to be sustainable.

He wrote a Medium post in 2019 titled “Reflecting on My Failure to Build a Billion-Dollar Company.” It got 1.5 million readers. Not because it was a failure story — because it was an honest story, and honest stories are rare enough online that they travel.

The Pivot to Sustainable

Sahil rebuilt Gumroad as a one-person company.

He automated what could be automated. He focused development on the things that actually moved the needle for creators. He stopped building features that were impressive to investors and started building features that were useful to sellers.

He made a radical decision: he would share the financials publicly. Revenue, churn, every number, posted for the world to see. Radical transparency as product. Radical transparency as trust.

In 2021, Gumroad processed $142 million in payments to creators. One person running it. He made enough to live well. He had time to paint — he had become a serious painter during the dark years — and write, and think.

The $8 million he’d raised and spent chasing billion-dollar metrics had delivered a company that nearly killed him. The company he rebuilt alone, without pressure, delivered everything he actually wanted.

What He Learned

“Build a company you want to run, not a company investors want to fund.”

This is the lesson Sahil Lavingia is most associated with now. It sounds obvious in retrospect. It is almost never obvious in the moment, when the pressure of outside expectations is so total that your own preferences feel like an indulgence you cannot afford.

He could afford them. He just didn’t know it yet.

The layoffs in January 2019 were the worst day of his professional life. They were also the day he stopped building someone else’s company and started building his own.

The Last Thing

Sahil Lavingia still runs Gumroad.

He still posts the numbers publicly. He still paints. He hired a small team, eventually — not because he had to, but because he chose to.

The Forbes 30 Under 30 is framed somewhere he no longer looks at.

The painting he finished last month is hanging in his living room.

He knows which one matters more.

Arvid Kahl: The Developer Who Sold His SaaS and Wrote the Book on How He Did It

The wire transfer confirmed at 11:23 AM on a Tuesday.

Arvid Kahl had just sold FeedbackPanda — a SaaS he had built with his partner — for an amount he wouldn’t disclose publicly but that was, he has said, “life-changing.”

He was thirty-five years old. He had been building the company for less than two years.

He closed his laptop and went for a walk. He did not know what to do with his hands.

The Problem That Became a Product

FeedbackPanda started as a tool Arvid’s partner needed.

She was an online English teacher — one of thousands of contractors working for Chinese EdTech companies, providing one-on-one English lessons to Chinese children via video call. The work was good but the administrative burden was crushing: after every lesson, teachers were required to write detailed feedback reports for each student’s parents.

Fifty lessons a week. Fifty feedback reports. Each one written from scratch.

She spent hours every week on feedback that the parents barely read. Arvid, watching this, did what developers do: he automated it.

FeedbackPanda was a template engine. Teachers could save sentence patterns, fill in student-specific details, and generate professional feedback in seconds instead of minutes. The time saving was not incremental — it was transformational. Teachers who had spent three hours a week on feedback now spent fifteen minutes.

He launched it to the community of online teachers. In the first month, he made $120.

In the second month, $800.

In the twelfth month, $30,000.

The Unexpected Moat

What Arvid Kahl understood — slowly, then completely — was that FeedbackPanda had a moat he hadn’t planned for: community.

Online English teachers were a tight-knit, self-organized group. They had Facebook groups, forums, referral networks. When one teacher found something that made their job easier, they told ten other teachers. When ten teachers loved it, a hundred followed.

He did not advertise. He did not run campaigns. He showed up in the communities, helped people, answered questions, and let the product speak through the teachers who used it.

“The best distribution channel for a B2C SaaS,” he wrote later, “is the community that already exists around the problem you’re solving.”

He had not created the community. He had found it, built something it needed, and then earned its trust by showing up consistently.

The Sale

When the EdTech market shifted — when the Chinese government cracked down on private tutoring companies in 2021, devastating the industry — Arvid had already sold FeedbackPanda in 2019.

The timing was lucky. But the decision to sell was deliberate.

He and his partner had built something real. They were at the point where growing it further would require either raising capital or hiring a team — both of which would change the nature of what they’d built and how they lived. They liked what they’d built. They liked how they lived. They sold.

The acquiring company could scale it. They would use the proceeds to figure out what came next.

What Came Next

What came next was a book.

Arvid Kahl spent two years writing The Embedded Entrepreneur — a systematic guide to finding, validating, and building products for communities. Then a second book. Then a newsletter, The Bootstrapped Founder, that grew to tens of thousands of readers.

The books were not passive income in the way people use that phrase dismissively. They were the result of hundreds of hours of writing, editing, thinking, revising. But they were also, once written, assets that generated revenue indefinitely.

By 2023, between the books, the newsletter, consulting, and speaking, Arvid Kahl was generating meaningful income from his writing alone — having never raised a dollar of venture capital, having built and sold one product and then pivoted entirely to teaching others how to do what he’d done.

The Lesson That Travels

Arvid Kahl’s core teaching is deceptively simple: before you build, find the community.

Not the market. The community.

A market is an abstraction. A community is people who already talk to each other, already have opinions, already have problems they’re actively trying to solve. Build for them and they will tell each other. Build for a market and you’ll spend everything you have on acquisition.

He found the community of online teachers because his partner was one of them. He didn’t have to research the pain. He lived next to it.

That proximity — that inside knowledge — was the unfair advantage that no amount of money could replicate.

The Wire Transfer

The wire transfer that confirmed at 11:23 AM on a Tuesday was the end of one story and the beginning of another.

He had built something real with someone he loved and sold it before the market that made it possible disappeared entirely.

He went for a walk. He did not know what to do with his hands.

Eventually he went home, opened his laptop, and started writing.

Some people don’t know when to stop. Arvid Kahl knew exactly when to start again.

Justin Welsh: The Burned-Out VP Who Built a $5M One-Person Business

In October 2019, Justin Welsh had a panic attack in a WeWork bathroom in Los Angeles.

He was the SVP of Sales at a healthcare startup. He was responsible for a team of fifty people and a revenue number in the tens of millions. He was, by the metrics his industry used, successful.

He was also done.

He sat on the bathroom floor for twenty minutes, texted his wife that he wasn’t okay, and drove home. He never went back to the office.

Before the Burnout

Justin Welsh had spent fifteen years building other people’s companies.

He was good at it — genuinely good, not just-good-enough good. He had a gift for sales, for building teams, for the particular blend of pressure and support that turns individual salespeople into systems that compound. He had climbed. He had earned. It also made failure cheap. A product that flopped had cost one month. That is a survivable loss. A product that flops after two years is a catastrophe that ends careers and marriages.

Andrey Azimov was, without using the language, running a portfolio strategy at high frequency.

The One That Worked

Among the twelve, one caught.

His products around the nomad and remote-work space found real traction, and one of them — a tool solving a genuine recurring problem for a specific group of people — started generating consistent revenue.

He kept it. He killed most of the others.

That is the correct behavior and almost nobody does it. Founders keep bad products alive out of sunk-cost attachment. He had built each one cheaply enough to be unsentimental about ending it.

Hardcore in Public

The public commitment mattered as much as the deadline.

He announced the challenge before he had a single product. He posted progress. He posted revenue screenshots showing $0. He posted when he almost missed a deadline.

The accountability was external and inescapable, which meant that on the nights when he did not feel like shipping, feeling was not a relevant variable.

This is the same mechanism as Ship 30 for 30, as building in public, as every effective creative constraint: remove the possibility of quietly quitting.

The Legacy

Andrey Azimov’s hardcore year became a template that other indie makers copied — the “12 startups in 12 months” challenge that dozens, then hundreds, of makers attempted.

Pieter Levels did a version of it. Marc Lou did a version of it. Both attribute part of their approach to the same underlying logic.

The logic is not complicated. Ship more things. Ship them faster. Kill the ones that don’t work. Keep the one that does.

The reason almost nobody does it is that it requires being publicly mediocre eleven times.

The Last Thing

Andrey Azimov shipped twelve products in twelve months and eleven of them didn’t matter.

The twelfth did.

He had no way of knowing in advance which one it would be — nobody does — so he built a system that guaranteed he would find out.

That is the whole method.

There is no other one.

Laura Roeder: The Social Media Consultant Who Escaped Her Own Success

Laura Roeder had a good problem.

Her social media consulting business was thriving. Clients wanted her. She was billing well. She was, by every external measure, successful.

She was also completely trapped.

Every dollar she earned required her presence. Every client required her attention. If she stopped working, income stopped immediately. She had built herself a very well-paid job, which is not the same thing as a business.

She decided to build software instead.

Edgar

Laura Roeder launched MeetEdgar in 2014.

The insight came directly from client work. Social media managers were spending hours scheduling posts, and then those posts would go out once and disappear forever. Evergreen content — a blog post that was just as relevant a year later — was published once and never resurfaced.

Edgar solved that specific thing: a library of your content, categorized, that recycled automatically. Schedule once, and Edgar would keep rotating it forever.

The name was deliberately friendly. Not “SocialSuite” or “PostFlow.” Edgar. Like a slightly incompetent but reliable assistant.

Bootstrapping With a Team

MeetEdgar was bootstrapped, but not solo.

Laura Roeder built a fully remote team from early on — unusual in 2014, when remote work was still treated as a compromise rather than a strategy.

She has written extensively about the operational side: how to hire remotely, how to run a company across time zones, how to build culture without an office, how to avoid the founder-as-bottleneck trap she had experienced in consulting.

The whole company was designed as the opposite of her consulting business. Revenue that did not require her presence. A team that could operate without her. Systems instead of heroics.

The Numbers

MeetEdgar reached over $4 million in annual recurring revenue.

No venture capital. A team of roughly thirty people, all remote. Profitable.

Laura Roeder stepped back from day-to-day operations, which was the point of the entire exercise. The business ran. She had built the thing she couldn’t build as a consultant: something that existed independently of her.

The Second Act

Laura Roeder later co-founded Paperbell — software for coaches, solving the business-operations side of a coaching practice: scheduling, payments, contracts, client management.

Same pattern. Take a group of people doing valuable work who are drowning in administrative overhead, and build the software that removes the overhead.

She had, by then, done it twice with the same underlying method: identify the operational pain in a service business you understand deeply, then build the product that automates it.

What She Argues About Ambition

Laura Roeder has been outspoken about a specific distortion in startup culture: the assumption that the goal is always maximum scale.

A $4 million profitable software company with thirty remote employees and a founder who works reasonable hours is, by venture standards, a failure. By any human standard, it is an extraordinary outcome.

“The definition of success is a choice,” she has argued. “Most founders never realize they made one.”

The Last Thing

Laura Roeder was very successful and completely trapped.

She built software specifically to escape the trap, and then she designed the software company so that it could not become a new trap.

That second decision is the one most founders skip.

They escape the job and build a company that is just a harder job.

She didn’t.

Ben Tossell: The Non-Coder Who Built a No-Code Empire

Ben Tossell could not code.

This was, for years, the fact that defined his relationship with the technology industry. He worked at Product Hunt. He was surrounded by builders. He watched people ship products constantly and could not participate.

He tried to learn. He did the tutorials. It did not stick — not because he was incapable, but because the gap between “I understand a for-loop” and “I can build the thing in my head” was so wide that motivation died in the middle of it.

Then he discovered the tools.

The No-Code Realization

Around 2018, a category of software was maturing that made it possible to build real applications without writing code: Bubble, Webflow, Zapier, Airtable, Integromat.

Ben Tossell started using them and discovered he could build things.

Not toy things. Real things. Marketplaces, directories, internal tools, automated workflows. Things that would have required a developer and a budget six months earlier.

The realization was not just personal. It was that a very large group of people — everyone with an idea and no engineering background — had just been handed the ability to build.

Nobody was teaching them.

Makerpad

Ben Tossell launched Makerpad in 2019 as a library of tutorials for building without code.

The format was specific: not “learn Bubble,” but “build an Airbnb clone,” “build a job board,” “automate your invoicing.” Outcome-oriented, project-based, showing the actual clicks.

It was a membership. People paid monthly for access.

It grew quickly, because the audience had no alternative. Traditional coding education was aimed at people becoming developers. Makerpad was aimed at people who wanted a specific thing to exist and did not care about becoming anything.

The Community Effect

Makerpad became more than tutorials.

The community — a Slack, then a broader network — filled with people who had built real businesses on no-code tools. They shared what worked. They hired each other. They validated each other’s approach at a time when the broader tech industry treated no-code as a toy.

That validation was the real product.

Being a non-technical founder in 2019 meant being told, constantly, that you needed a technical co-founder. Makerpad’s community was thousands of people proving that was no longer true.

The Zapier Acquisition

In 2021, Zapier acquired Makerpad.

The strategic logic was clear: Zapier was infrastructure for exactly the audience Makerpad had assembled and educated. The acquisition brought both the content library and the community into Zapier’s orbit.

Ben Tossell — the guy who couldn’t code — sold his company to one of the most important automation platforms in the industry.

Ben’s Bites

After Makerpad, Ben Tossell did it again, in a different domain.

When large language models started becoming broadly useful in late 2022, he launched Ben’s Bites — a daily newsletter about AI.

The playbook was identical to Makerpad: identify a technology that is about to matter enormously, notice that non-experts have no accessible on-ramp, build the on-ramp, build the community, monetize the attention.

Ben’s Bites grew to over 100,000 subscribers within months and became one of the most-read AI newsletters in existence.

He had proven the method was repeatable.

The Last Thing

Ben Tossell could not code, and for years that felt like a permanent limitation.

It turned out to be his qualification.

He built the definitive resource for non-coders because he was one — because he knew exactly which explanations landed and which assumed knowledge his audience didn’t have.

The developers who understood the tools better could not have built Makerpad.

They had forgotten what not knowing feels like.

Paul Jarvis: The Designer Who Argued That Growth Is a Choice

Paul Jarvis spent years as a web designer for large clients.

Mercedes-Benz. Microsoft. Warner Music. Yahoo. The kind of client list that designers build careers around and put at the top of their portfolios forever.

He walked away from all of it.

Not because the work was bad. Because the life around the work was bad — the meetings, the scale, the account managers, the sense that his time was being spent on things that would be replaced in eighteen months.

He moved to a small island off the coast of British Columbia and started working alone.

Company of One

Paul Jarvis’s central argument, eventually published as the book Company of One, is deceptively simple: growth is not automatically good.

Every business assumption points the other way. Growth is the metric. Growth is the goal. A company that isn’t growing is dying.

His counterargument: growth introduces complexity, and complexity introduces cost — in management overhead, in coordination, in stress, in the erosion of the reasons you started. A one-person business that earns $200,000 and requires thirty hours a week is, for most people, a better outcome than a twenty-person business that earns $2 million and requires eighty.

The question is not “how do I grow” but “what is enough, and what does it cost to get there?”

Almost nobody asks the second question.

The Products

Paul Jarvis did not write a book about small business and then have no business.

He built products: courses on freelancing and design, books, and — most substantially — Fathom Analytics, a privacy-focused alternative to Google Analytics, co-founded with Jack Ellis.

Fathom is the practical demonstration of the philosophy. It competes with a Google product. It has two founders and a tiny team. It does not raise money. It does not chase enterprise contracts requiring a sales organization.

It is profitable and growing and small on purpose.

The Privacy Bet

Fathom Analytics succeeded on a positioning bet that looked risky in 2018 and looks obvious now.

Google Analytics was free, dominant, and universal. Competing with free is generally a losing proposition.

But Google Analytics was free because the product was the data, and the customer was Google. Website owners were increasingly uncomfortable with that — especially after GDPR made the legal exposure concrete.

Fathom charged money and collected no personal data. Simple dashboard, no cookie banner required, no user tracking.

It turned out a meaningful number of people would pay for software that did not surveil their visitors.

Living the Argument

What distinguishes Paul Jarvis from most people who write about work-life philosophy is that he actually lives on the island.

He works limited hours. He does not scale. He turns down opportunities that would require hiring. He has said no to acquisition conversations, to expansion, to the standard next steps.

He has been doing this for over a decade, publicly, with real revenue.

The argument is not theoretical. It is a functioning demonstration that has now run long enough to be evidence rather than opinion.

The Last Thing

Paul Jarvis had a client list that most designers would trade years for.

He left it to work alone on a small island, and then spent a decade arguing — with a book, with a business, with his actual daily life — that the thing everyone is chasing might not be worth catching.

The most radical sentence in Company of One is the simplest one:

“What if the goal is not more?”

Nobody had asked him that. So he asked it himself, and then he built a life around the answer.

Peter Askew: The Man Who Bought a Domain Name and Accidentally Sold Vidalia Onions

Peter Askew bought a domain name.

The domain was vidaliaonions.com. He paid a few thousand dollars for it at auction, on instinct, without a business plan.

He was a domain investor. Buying names was what he did. Most of them sat idle, occasionally sold, occasionally didn’t.

But this one bothered him. It was too good to sit idle. Vidalia onions are a real, protected, geographically-specific agricultural product from Georgia, with a genuine season and genuine fans.

Somebody should be selling them at that domain.

So he drove to Georgia and found a farmer.

The Cold Call to a Farm

Peter Askew had no experience in agriculture, logistics, food safety, or perishable shipping.

He drove to Vidalia, Georgia, and started asking around for a farmer who might be interested in an internet partnership.

The reasonable expectation is that this goes nowhere. A stranger from out of state shows up asking about selling onions online.

He found a farmer. They made a deal. The farmer would grow and ship. Peter would run the website, handle the marketing, and manage the orders.

The first season worked.

The Economics of an Absurd Business

The Vidalia onion business is a spectacular example of the value of an exact-match domain.

When onion season arrives and someone searches for “vidalia onions,” Peter Askew’s site is the answer. Not because of an SEO agency. Because the domain is literally the search term and the site sells the thing.

Customer acquisition cost: approximately zero.

The business generates several hundred thousand dollars per season, concentrated in a few months of the year, with a business model that consists of: own the right domain, partner with the right producer, don’t screw up the shipping.

Deep South Rides and Other Absurdities

Peter Askew’s other businesses follow the same logic, and each is stranger than the last.

He owns and operates businesses built around domains he acquired: a horse-riding directory, a dude-ranch booking site, various niche properties in categories nobody would deliberately choose.

He calls the approach “domain-driven entrepreneurship” — starting with the asset and then discovering what business it implies, rather than starting with a business idea and then acquiring assets.

It is completely backwards from every startup methodology.

It works because search intent is a real, measurable, monetizable thing, and owning the exact phrase that expresses an intent is a durable advantage that nobody can copy.

Why This Story Matters

Peter Askew’s story is included in this book because it breaks the pattern of every other story in it.

He did not have a personal pain point. He was not scratching his own itch. He had never grown an onion, ridden a horse professionally, or worked in agriculture.

He had an asset and a willingness to drive to Georgia and knock on doors.

The lesson is not “buy domains.” The lesson is that opportunity is not always found by introspection. Sometimes it is found by noticing that a valuable thing is sitting unused and being the person willing to do the unglamorous logistics of putting it to work.

The Last Thing

Peter Askew bought a domain name about onions because it seemed like a waste to leave it parked.

Then he drove to Georgia and found a farmer.

Everything else — the seasons, the revenue, the strange portfolio of businesses about horses and produce — followed from a man who could not tolerate the idea of a perfectly good domain name doing nothing.

He is the only person in this book whose entire career began with an aesthetic objection to waste.

Monica Lent: The Engineering Manager Who Bootstrapped to $10k MRR in a Year

Monica Lent was an engineering manager in Berlin, doing well, and quietly running an experiment.

Nobody could tell what was working.

Affilimate

Affilimate unified them. Connect your affiliate accounts, connect your site analytics, and see revenue attributed to specific pages, specific links, specific placements.

Monica Lent built Affilimate while working, then went full-time.

High-value customers with an obvious ROI story. The best kind of market.

The Last Thing

Monica Lent was an engineering manager in Berlin who wrote blog posts on the side and got annoyed that she couldn’t tell which ones were making money.

The annoyance became a product. The blog became the distribution channel. The engineering background made the hard part possible.

Every piece of her advantage came from something she was already doing for other reasons.

That is how most of these stories actually work.

The pieces are already there. The founder is the person who notices they fit together.

Adam Wathan: The Developer Who Made a CSS Framework People Argued About

Adam Wathan wrote a blog post that made a lot of developers angry.

The post argued that the conventional wisdom about CSS — semantic class names, separation of concerns, avoiding inline-style-like utility classes — was wrong in practice, whatever its merits in theory.

He argued for utility-first CSS: small, single-purpose classes composed directly in the markup. flex, pt-4, text-center, bg-blue-500.

To a certain kind of developer, this was heresy. It looked like inline styles. It looked like everything CSS best practices had spent a decade telling people not to do.

The post was widely shared, widely criticized, and widely — quietly — agreed with by people who had been feeling the same thing without saying it.

Tailwind

Adam Wathan built Tailwind CSS with Steve Schoger and a small group of collaborators, releasing it in 2017.

It was the framework version of the argument. Utilities, composable, configurable, with a design system baked in.

Adoption was slow at first and then extremely fast.

By 2023, Tailwind was one of the most widely used CSS frameworks in the world, used by hundreds of thousands of developers and by major companies including OpenAI, Shopify, and GitHub.

The framework was free and open source.

The Business Question

Free open source frameworks are famously difficult to monetize. Most maintainers burn out, unpaid, supporting projects used by billion-dollar companies.

Adam Wathan solved this in a way that has since been widely imitated.

He did not monetize the framework. He monetized the thing the framework’s users wanted next: professionally designed components.

Tailwind UI launched in 2020 — a library of pre-built, professionally designed component templates for Tailwind, sold as a one-time purchase.

First-week revenue: over $1 million.

The framework was the funnel. The framework was free forever, genuinely, with no rug-pull and no open-core bait-and-switch. The component library was the product.

Why It Worked

The Tailwind UI model worked because of a specific alignment.

The people who most needed pre-built components were the people already using Tailwind heavily — professional developers building real applications on deadlines, employed by companies with budgets.

The product was expensive enough ($149 to $299 initially) to be a real business and cheap enough to be an obvious expense against developer time.

And critically: buying Tailwind UI did not gate anything. Everything you could build with it, you could build without it, slower. The purchase bought time, not access.

That distinction preserved the trust that made the free framework spread in the first place.

The Courses Before

Before Tailwind, Adam Wathan had built a business selling programming courses — Test-Driven Laravel, Refactoring to Collections, Advanced Vue Component Design.

These were expensive, deep, technically rigorous courses aimed at professionals. They generated substantial revenue.

The courses funded the years of open-source work that became Tailwind. And they taught him the audience-building mechanics — writing, screencasting, launching to a list — that he later applied at much larger scale.

Nothing appeared from nowhere. Each stage funded and taught the next.

The Last Thing

Adam Wathan wrote a blog post arguing that a widely-held best practice was wrong.

He was willing to be told he was wrong, in public, by a lot of people, for a long time.

Then he built the tool that proved the argument, gave it away, and sold the thing that people wanted once they agreed with him.

The framework has hundreds of thousands of users. The business does eight figures.

It started with being willing to say the unpopular thing and then do the work to back it up.

That order matters. Saying it is free. Backing it up took six years.

Jesse Hanley: The Maker Who Built Bento From a Dozen Failures

Jesse Hanley had shipped a lot of things that didn’t work.

Not spectacular failures — quiet ones. Products launched to indifference. Ideas that had seemed obviously good in the planning stage and turned out to have no buyers. Weeks and months of work absorbed by things that generated a handful of signups and then nothing.

This is the most common experience in indie software and the least documented, because nobody writes a retrospective about a product that made $40.

He kept shipping anyway.

The Pattern Under the Failures

Looking back across the failed products, Jesse Hanley noticed something.

Every one of them had needed the same infrastructure: email capture, user tracking, behavioral triggers, automated sequences. Every time, he had cobbled it together from a marketing tool that was either too expensive, too limited, or too clearly built for a different kind of business.

The existing options were built for large marketing teams at established companies. They were priced for those teams, structured for those workflows, and full of features that a solo maker would never touch while missing the ones they needed daily.

He had experienced this a dozen times without recognizing it as a signal.

Bento

Bento launched as a marketing automation platform built for the way software businesses actually work.

Not “email marketing” in the newsletter sense. Behavioral automation: track what users do inside your product, trigger messages based on that behavior, connect the data to the messaging without requiring an integration project.

The pitch was to a specific person: the technical founder running a SaaS business who needed real automation and did not want to pay enterprise marketing prices or hire someone to operate it.

Jesse Hanley knew that person precisely, because he had been that person a dozen times.

Building for a Person You’ve Been

The design decisions in Bento reflect a founder who has felt the specific frustrations.

Developer-first APIs, because the intended user can write code. Transparent pricing, because the intended user has been burned by “contact sales.” Automation logic that is powerful rather than simplified, because the intended user does not need training wheels.

None of these are obvious from market research. They come from having been the customer.

The Long Middle

Bento did not explode. It grew.

Jesse Hanley has been candid about the years of steady, unglamorous building — support tickets, infrastructure work, incremental improvements, slow customer growth compounding month over month.

There was no Product Hunt moment that changed everything. There was a long sequence of months where the number went up slightly.

This is the actual texture of most successful bootstrapped software, and it is almost never the story that gets told, because “we grew 4% this month for forty consecutive months” makes a terrible headline and an excellent business.

What the Failures Were For

The dozen failed products were not wasted.

They were the market research. Each one taught something about what customers actually pay for, what they say they want versus what they use, how much work a launch requires, how quickly indifference sets in.

More importantly, they produced the observation that became Bento — an observation that was only visible after building the same infrastructure a dozen times.

You cannot see a pattern from one instance.

The Last Thing

Jesse Hanley shipped a dozen things that didn’t work.

The thirteenth worked because the first twelve had shown him, through repetition, what was missing every single time.

The failures were not the price of success. They were the mechanism of it.

Nobody wants to hear that. It is true anyway.

John Rush: The Founder Who Runs Twenty Products at Once

John Rush does something that violates every piece of startup advice ever given.

He runs many products simultaneously.

Not sequentially. Not a portfolio he acquired and delegated. Actively, concurrently, as a solo founder with minimal help: directories, AI tools, website builders, SEO products, marketplaces — twenty or more live products generating revenue at the same time.

The conventional advice is unambiguous: focus. Do one thing. Kill everything else. Every accelerator, every mentor, every book says the same thing.

He does the opposite and it works.

Why Focus Is Advice For Someone Else

The focus doctrine comes from venture capital, and it makes sense in that context.

If you have raised money on the promise of a billion-dollar outcome, splitting attention across twenty small products is malpractice. You need one enormous winner and every hour spent elsewhere reduces the probability of getting it.

But John Rush is not trying to build a billion-dollar company. He is trying to maximize total revenue across a portfolio while remaining independent.

Under that objective, concentration is a bug, not a feature.

Twenty products earning $2,000/month each is $40,000/month, with no single point of failure, no dependence on any one market remaining favorable, and no scenario where a Google algorithm change or a platform policy shift destroys everything.

The Systems That Make It Possible

Running twenty products alone is only possible with extreme systematization.

John Rush reuses infrastructure across products relentlessly. Shared authentication, shared billing, shared analytics, shared deployment pipelines, shared component libraries. Launching product twenty-one costs a fraction of what launching product one cost, because most of it already exists.

He also builds products that require minimal support by design. Self-serve onboarding. Documentation instead of email. Simple enough that users don’t get stuck.

The constraint shapes the product selection. He does not build things that require hand-holding, because he cannot hold hands twenty times.

The Marketing Machine

The other half of the system is distribution.

John Rush builds heavily in the SEO and directory space — products that acquire customers through search rather than through active selling. Once ranking, they generate signups without ongoing effort.

He also cross-promotes across his own portfolio. Each product links to the others. Each launch reaches an audience built by the previous ones. The portfolio compounds because the products feed each other traffic.

This is a structural advantage that a focused single-product founder cannot access.

The Failure Rate

Not every product works. Most don’t, at least not well.

This is fine, structurally, because the cost of building product twenty-one is low. A product that fails costs a few weeks of a founder who has already built the reusable infrastructure.

The math is the same as venture capital, applied at a personal scale: many small bets, most fail, the winners cover everything.

He is running a fund where he is the only portfolio company and also all of them.

The Last Thing

John Rush runs twenty products at once and is told constantly that he should focus.

The advice is correct for the person giving it and wrong for him.

The lesson is not “run twenty products.” The lesson is that almost all business advice is downstream of an objective function that was never stated out loud — and if your objective is different, the advice inverts.

Ask what the advisor is optimizing for.

Then ask whether it is the same thing you are.
