# Laura Roeder: The Social Media Consultant Who Escaped Her Own Success

Laura Roeder had a good problem.

Her social media consulting business was thriving. Clients wanted her. She was billing well. She was, by every external measure, successful.

She was also completely trapped.

Every dollar she earned required her presence. Every client required her attention. If she stopped working, income stopped immediately. She had built herself a very well-paid job, which is not the same thing as a business.

She decided to build software instead.

---

## Edgar

Laura Roeder launched MeetEdgar in 2014.

The insight came directly from client work. Social media managers were spending hours scheduling posts, and then those posts would go out once and disappear forever. Evergreen content — a blog post that was just as relevant a year later — was published once and never resurfaced.

Edgar solved that specific thing: a library of your content, categorized, that recycled automatically. Schedule once, and Edgar would keep rotating it forever.

The name was deliberately friendly. Not "SocialSuite" or "PostFlow." Edgar. Like a slightly incompetent but reliable assistant.

---

## Bootstrapping With a Team

MeetEdgar was bootstrapped, but not solo.

Laura Roeder built a fully remote team from early on — unusual in 2014, when remote work was still treated as a compromise rather than a strategy.

She has written extensively about the operational side: how to hire remotely, how to run a company across time zones, how to build culture without an office, how to avoid the founder-as-bottleneck trap she had experienced in consulting.

The whole company was designed as the opposite of her consulting business. Revenue that did not require her presence. A team that could operate without her. Systems instead of heroics.

---

## The Numbers

MeetEdgar reached over $4 million in annual recurring revenue.

No venture capital. A team of roughly thirty people, all remote. Profitable.

Laura Roeder stepped back from day-to-day operations, which was the point of the entire exercise. The business ran. She had built the thing she couldn't build as a consultant: something that existed independently of her.

---

## The Second Act

Laura Roeder later co-founded Paperbell — software for coaches, solving the business-operations side of a coaching practice: scheduling, payments, contracts, client management.

Same pattern. Take a group of people doing valuable work who are drowning in administrative overhead, and build the software that removes the overhead.

She had, by then, done it twice with the same underlying method: identify the operational pain in a service business you understand deeply, then build the product that automates it.

---

## What She Argues About Ambition

Laura Roeder has been outspoken about a specific distortion in startup culture: the assumption that the goal is always maximum scale.

A $4 million profitable software company with thirty remote employees and a founder who works reasonable hours is, by venture standards, a failure. By any human standard, it is an extraordinary outcome.

"The definition of success is a choice," she has argued. "Most founders never realize they made one."

---

## The Last Thing

Laura Roeder was very successful and completely trapped.

She built software specifically to escape the trap, and then she designed the software company so that it could not become a new trap.

That second decision is the one most founders skip.

They escape the job and build a company that is just a harder job.

She didn't.
