# Alex Hormozi: The Gym Owner Who Lost Everything Twice Before Making $100M

In 2016, Alex Hormozi had $1,036 in his bank account.

He had owned six gyms. He had sold them. He had taken the proceeds and put them into a new venture that was, at that moment, actively failing. He was sleeping on the floor of a gym in a sleeping bag because he could not afford both rent and payroll and had chosen payroll.

He was twenty-six.

---

## The First Collapse

Alex Hormozi's first business was a gym. Then five more gyms. He built them fast, ran them well, and sold them.

The proceeds went into Gym Launch — a business that would help other gym owners fill their gyms. The model was aggressive: he and his team would fly to a gym, run a promotional campaign, fill the gym with members, and take a cut.

It worked, and then it didn't. Expansion outran cash flow. A payment processor froze the account. Money that was supposed to arrive did not arrive.

He lost everything. Twice, by his own accounting — once with the gyms, once with the initial version of Gym Launch.

The sleeping bag on the gym floor was not a metaphor.

---

## The Rebuild

The rebuild of Gym Launch was structurally different.

Instead of running campaigns for gym owners, he taught them to run their own. He licensed the system. Gym owners paid to learn the playbook and to be part of a community that shared what was working.

The economics inverted. Instead of a service business that scaled with headcount, it was an information business that scaled with distribution.

Gym Launch went from near-zero to over $30 million in annual revenue within a few years.

---

## Acquisition.com

By 2020, Alex Hormozi had done well enough that he faced the standard question: what now?

His answer was Acquisition.com — a portfolio company that takes equity positions in existing businesses and helps them scale, using the operational playbooks he had developed.

The portfolio's aggregate revenue reportedly exceeded $200 million annually.

But the more visible thing he built was the content.

---

## $100M Offers

In 2021, Alex Hormozi published *$100M Offers*, a book about constructing offers that are so good that prospects feel stupid saying no.

He priced it at approximately the cost of shipping — a few dollars.

This was a deliberate, unusual move. Most business authors price for margin. He priced for distribution. He wanted the book in as many hands as possible because the book was the top of a funnel that ended at Acquisition.com.

The book sold hundreds of thousands of copies. Then *$100M Leads* did the same.

The content — books, YouTube, podcasts, social clips — became the largest business development engine he had. He gives away, publicly and in enormous volume, material that other consultants charge five figures for.

The logic is straightforward: the free content proves competence at scale, and the people it attracts are pre-qualified.

---

## The Actual Lesson

Alex Hormozi's most-repeated advice is about volume.

Not clever tactics. Volume. Do more of the thing. Make more offers, send more emails, post more content, have more conversations. Most people fail not because their strategy is wrong but because they execute it three times and conclude it doesn't work.

"You don't have a strategy problem," he has said. "You have a volume problem."

He is a man who lost everything twice and rebuilt twice, so he speaks with the specific authority of someone who has tested whether persistence actually pays.

---

## The Last Thing

In 2016, Alex Hormozi had $1,036 and a sleeping bag on a gym floor.

By 2023, his portfolio was doing hundreds of millions in revenue and his books had reached millions of readers.

He is unusually blunt about the fact that the gap between those two points was not luck or genius but the willingness to do unglamorous work for years while nobody was watching, after having already failed publicly.

Failing publicly and continuing anyway is the rarest skill in business.

It is also, in his telling, the only one that mattered.
