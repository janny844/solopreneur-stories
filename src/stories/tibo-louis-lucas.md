# Tibo Louis-Lucas: The Frenchman Who Built in Public Until the Internet Paid Attention

Tibo Louis-Lucas posted every day for 365 days before anything significant happened.

He was building TweetHunter — a Twitter growth tool — with his co-founder. He was also building an audience on Twitter, documenting the process publicly: the revenue numbers, the feature decisions, the arguments he and his co-founder had about product direction.

On day one, he had 800 followers.

On day 365, he had 60,000.

By the time he sold TweetHunter eighteen months later, the product was generating $1.6 million in annual recurring revenue.

The audience had become the distribution. The distribution had become the business.

---

## The Accidental Discovery

Tibo Louis-Lucas did not plan to become a build-in-public creator.

He planned to build a product that made money.

The building-in-public started because he was frustrated. He had ideas. He had technical skills. He had launched things before that went nowhere. He couldn't understand why some products found audiences and others didn't.

So he decided to document everything — the thinking, the decisions, the pivots — in the hope that someone who knew what he was doing wrong would tell him.

Someone did. Many someones did.

The feedback was real and specific in a way that private customer interviews rarely are, because it came from people who were watching the whole picture — not just responding to a targeted question but reacting to the full story.

He started building differently because people watching told him where he was wrong.

---

## TweetHunter

TweetHunter was built to solve the problem that Tibo and his co-founder Loic Lannelongue had themselves: growing on Twitter was opaque and slow.

They built tools to analyze which tweets performed, to find viral tweet formats, to schedule content, to track follower growth. They built it for themselves, launched it to the audience Tibo had built, and priced it at $19/month.

First month: $8,000 MRR.

They had validated the product in the market they had built around themselves.

By month six: $50,000 MRR.

By month twelve: $130,000 MRR.

---

## The Acquisition

In 2022, Taplio and TweetHunter were acquired by Lemlist for $4 million.

Tibo was twenty-six years old.

He stayed on after the acquisition to continue developing the product. He kept posting on Twitter. He kept building in public.

"The acquisition changed my financial life," he said. "It didn't change how I work, which is the part I actually like."

---

## What Build-in-Public Actually Means

People misunderstand build-in-public as marketing — as posting revenue screenshots for attention.

Tibo Louis-Lucas's version of it is different. It is accountability, feedback loop, and distribution rolled into one practice.

**Accountability:** When you've told 60,000 people what you're building and why, the cost of quitting is higher than it would be otherwise. This is not peer pressure in the negative sense — it's a commitment device that works.

**Feedback loop:** The people who follow your build will tell you when something is wrong faster and more honestly than any user interview. Because they're not being asked — they're reacting.

**Distribution:** When you launch to an audience that has watched you build, they feel ownership over the outcome. They want you to succeed because they've been part of the story. This translates into word-of-mouth that no ad budget can replicate.

---

## The Last Thing

365 days. 800 followers to 60,000. A product from zero to $1.6 million ARR.

Tibo Louis-Lucas did not build something secretly and then reveal it.

He built something publicly and let the building be the story.

The internet rewarded the honesty. Not immediately. Not on day 47, when the numbers were embarrassing and the path forward wasn't clear. But eventually, and then all at once.

That's how compounding works. It's boring until it isn't.
