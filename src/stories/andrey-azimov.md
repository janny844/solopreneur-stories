# Andrey Azimov: The Developer Who Shipped a Product Every Month Until One Stuck

Andrey Azimov made a public commitment in 2017.

He would ship one product every month for a year. Publicly. With a deadline. No exceptions, no extensions, no "it's not ready yet."

He called it his "hardcore year."

He had almost no money, a laptop, and a growing conviction that the only difference between people who built successful products and people who didn't was that the first group actually finished things.

---

## The Products

The list of what he shipped is a study in range and in ruthless scoping.

A Mac app that made your computer beep when your Bitcoin hit a threshold. A tool for finding a place to live as a nomad. A game. A tiny utility. A directory. A Chrome extension.

Some took two weeks. Some took the full month. Most made almost no money.

He shipped them anyway, on schedule, and posted the results — including revenue, including zero — every time.

---

## The Mechanics of the Constraint

The one-month deadline was doing specific work.

It made scope decisions automatic. If a feature couldn't fit in the remaining weeks, it wasn't in the product. There was no negotiation, no "just one more sprint," no gradual expansion of an idea until it became unbuildable.

It also made failure cheap. A product that flopped had cost one month. That is a survivable loss. A product that flops after two years is a catastrophe that ends careers and marriages.

And it produced volume. Twelve products in a year meant twelve independent chances at something working — versus one carefully-planned product with one chance.

Andrey Azimov was, without using the language, running a portfolio strategy at high frequency.

---

## The One That Worked

Among the twelve, one caught.

His products around the nomad and remote-work space found real traction, and one of them — a tool solving a genuine recurring problem for a specific group of people — started generating consistent revenue.

He kept it. He killed most of the others.

That is the correct behavior and almost nobody does it. Founders keep bad products alive out of sunk-cost attachment. He had built each one cheaply enough to be unsentimental about ending it.

---

## Hardcore in Public

The public commitment mattered as much as the deadline.

He announced the challenge before he had a single product. He posted progress. He posted revenue screenshots showing $0. He posted when he almost missed a deadline.

The accountability was external and inescapable, which meant that on the nights when he did not feel like shipping, feeling was not a relevant variable.

This is the same mechanism as Ship 30 for 30, as building in public, as every effective creative constraint: remove the possibility of quietly quitting.

---

## The Legacy

Andrey Azimov's hardcore year became a template that other indie makers copied — the "12 startups in 12 months" challenge that dozens, then hundreds, of makers attempted.

Pieter Levels did a version of it. Marc Lou did a version of it. Both attribute part of their approach to the same underlying logic.

The logic is not complicated. Ship more things. Ship them faster. Kill the ones that don't work. Keep the one that does.

The reason almost nobody does it is that it requires being publicly mediocre eleven times.

---

## The Last Thing

Andrey Azimov shipped twelve products in twelve months and eleven of them didn't matter.

The twelfth did.

He had no way of knowing in advance which one it would be — nobody does — so he built a system that guaranteed he would find out.

That is the whole method.

There is no other one.
