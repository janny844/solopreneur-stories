# Damon Chen: The Developer Who Built a $3M Business From One Underserved Problem

Damon Chen launched Testimonial.to in 2021 with a landing page, a Stripe integration, and one core belief: collecting customer testimonials was painful enough that people would pay to make it simple.

He was right.

Twelve months later, Testimonial.to was generating $30,000 in monthly recurring revenue.

Alone. No co-founder, no employees, no outside investment.

---

## The Problem

The problem Damon Chen solved is one that every business owner has encountered and nobody had made elegant.

You know your customers are happy. You want to show potential customers that your customers are happy. But getting actual testimonials — written, video, with a photo, formatted for a website — is a friction-filled process that requires chasing people, formatting responses manually, and updating your website whenever something new comes in.

Most businesses either have no testimonials or have three testimonials from three years ago from customers who might not even be customers anymore.

Testimonial.to was a link you sent. Customers clicked it, recorded a video or wrote text, and their testimonial appeared on a hosted wall that you could embed on your website in one line of code.

That was the whole product at launch.

---

## The Launch

Damon Chen posted Testimonial.to on Product Hunt in April 2021.

He had prepared the listing carefully — screenshots, a short demo video, a clear description of the problem and the solution. He had an email list of zero people and a Twitter following of a few thousand.

Product Hunt front page: yes.

Revenue on launch day: $2,400.

This was real validation, not just traffic. People were paying, the same day they discovered it, for a product that solved a problem they recognized immediately.

He had found a real problem and built a clean solution and put it in front of the right people.

---

## What He Did After

Damon Chen's post-launch behavior is worth studying because it is the opposite of what most founders do after a successful launch.

He did not add features frantically. He did not raise prices immediately. He did not hire.

He listened.

He read every support ticket. He read every feature request. He tracked where users dropped off in the onboarding. He identified the specific friction points — the places where the tool was almost good enough but not quite — and he fixed them.

The product became better, specifically better, in response to the people who were already using it.

MRR went from $2,400 in month one to $10,000 by month four. Not because he added features. Because the features he had started working better.

---

## The Positioning Win

Testimonial.to succeeded not just because it was useful but because it was positioned correctly.

"Collect video and text testimonials easily" — the tagline was not clever. It was accurate. It described exactly what the product did for exactly the person who needed it.

In a world full of marketing that describes products in terms of outcomes ("transform your brand," "drive conversions") rather than actions ("collect testimonials"), clarity is a competitive advantage.

When someone Googles "how to collect testimonials from customers," Testimonial.to is the answer. Not because of elaborate SEO strategy — because the product name and description match what people are searching for.

---

## The $3M Business

By 2023, Testimonial.to was processing enough transactions to generate several million dollars in annual recurring revenue.

Damon Chen had by then hired a small team — customer support, some part-time help with marketing. But the product remained focused. It still did what it said: collected testimonials. It had more formats, more integrations, more polish.

Not more things. More of the same thing, done better.

---

## The Last Thing

Damon Chen built a $3 million business from one underserved problem.

Not by disrupting an industry. By looking at something that was annoying — collecting customer testimonials — and making it less annoying.

The market for "less annoying" is larger than it looks. Because most things are still annoying. And the developers who find them, build the solution, and charge a fair price for making them less so will keep building real businesses from what everyone else is too comfortable to notice.
