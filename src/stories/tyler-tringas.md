# Tyler Tringas: The Man Who Built a Business From a Beach and Then Funded Others

Tyler Tringas built Storemapper while traveling.

Not in a startup office. Not in an accelerator. On laptops in cafes across South America and Southeast Asia, in the years before "digital nomad" was a category anyone recognized as legitimate.

The product was almost boring: a store locator widget for e-commerce sites. If you sold a physical product in retail stores, Storemapper let you put a searchable map on your website showing where customers could buy it.

That was it. That was the whole product.

It made him a millionaire.

---

## The Beauty of Boring

Storemapper succeeded because it was unglamorous.

No investor was interested in a store locator widget. No competitor with venture funding was going to attack the market, because the market was too small to justify a Series A.

But the market was not too small to support one person.

Thousands of e-commerce businesses needed a store locator. They needed it to work. They did not need it to be innovative. Tyler Tringas charged them $15 to $80 per month, and the recurring revenue compounded quietly for years.

He has called this the "micro-SaaS" model — deliberately choosing markets too small for venture-backed competitors and too valuable to ignore.

---

## The Operating Constraints

Tyler Tringas ran Storemapper with a set of self-imposed rules.

No employees for as long as possible. No fundraising. No features that would meaningfully increase support load. No expansion into adjacent markets that would double the surface area of the product.

The goal was not maximum revenue. The goal was maximum revenue per hour of his attention.

He optimized ruthlessly for automation. Onboarding self-served. Support was mostly documentation. Billing was Stripe. The product was designed so that a new customer could go from signup to working widget without ever contacting him.

At its peak, Storemapper generated meaningful monthly revenue while requiring a few hours a week.

---

## The Sale

Tyler Tringas sold Storemapper in 2018.

The reported price was several million dollars — an extraordinary outcome for a store locator widget built by one person on a laptop in a series of cafes.

The buyer got a clean, well-documented, low-maintenance business with years of stable recurring revenue.

He got the capital and the freedom to do the thing he actually wanted to do next.

---

## Earnest Capital

What Tyler Tringas did next is the more interesting part of the story.

He founded Earnest Capital — a fund that invests in bootstrapped businesses.

The problem he was solving: founders building sustainable, profitable, non-venture-scale businesses had no funding options. Venture capital required a swing for a billion-dollar outcome. Bank loans required collateral and profitability that early-stage companies didn't have. There was nothing in between.

Earnest Capital created a new instrument — the Shared Earnings Agreement — that gave founders capital without requiring an exit. Investors got returns from profit distributions rather than from an acquisition.

It was a financial product designed by someone who had actually built the kind of company it was meant to serve.

---

## The Philosophy

Tyler Tringas's writing on "the calm company" and micro-SaaS has become foundational reading in the bootstrapper community.

The core argument: growth-at-all-costs is one strategy among many, it is optimized for a specific investor return profile, and it is almost never the right strategy for a founder who wants a good life rather than a chance at a billion dollars.

Most founders never consciously choose. They absorb the venture narrative because it is the only narrative available.

He spent his career building an alternative and then funding it.

---

## The Last Thing

Tyler Tringas built a store locator widget on a laptop in cafes across three continents.

It made him millions.

Then he took the millions and built the funding infrastructure that would have made his own path easier — for everyone coming after him.

That is the rarest thing in this book: someone who made it out and then went back to hold the door.
