# Nathan Barry: The Designer Who Built ConvertKit by Ignoring Every Rule

In 2013, Nathan Barry set a goal: make $5,000 in one month from information products.

He did it. He made $12,000.

Then he spent the next three years building a company that almost killed him financially, before it became the thing that made him: ConvertKit, an email marketing platform for creators that now processes over $30 million in annual recurring revenue.

The distance between $12,000 in a month and $30 million a year is not a straight line. It is a series of wrong turns navigated by someone too stubborn to stop.

---

## The First Chapter

Nathan Barry was a designer. A good one — he had worked on apps, consulted for startups, built iOS apps that did well. He understood interfaces, he understood user experience, he understood the gap between what software looked like and what it felt like to use.

In 2012, he wrote a book about app design. He sold it himself, directly to his audience, without a publisher. He made $12,000 in the first month.

This was revelatory.

Not the money, exactly — though the money was real. The revelation was structural: he had created something once and sold it many times. His effort was fixed. His revenue was not.

He wrote two more books. They sold well. He was making a comfortable living as a one-person information product creator when he made the decision that would nearly ruin him.

He decided to build software.

---

## The Bet

The bet was ConvertKit: an email marketing platform designed specifically for bloggers and creators.

The market already had players — MailChimp, AWeber, Constant Contact. They were generic tools for generic businesses. Nathan Barry believed there was a product to be built for the specific needs of people who taught things online, who wrote newsletters, who built audiences as their primary business model.

He started building in 2013.

In February 2014, he had $1,500 in monthly recurring revenue.

At that rate, the business would take years to become meaningful. He had spent money building it. He had a family. He had obligations.

He almost shut it down.

---

## The Public Commitment

Instead of shutting it down, Nathan Barry did something that sounds either brave or reckless depending on your risk tolerance: he posted publicly that he would grow ConvertKit to $5,000/month in MRR or shut it down.

He set a deadline. He told the internet.

This is a forcing function disguised as accountability. By making the commitment public, he made quitting more costly than it would have been otherwise — quitting now meant explaining failure to the people who had been watching.

He got to $5,000/month.

Then he set $10,000/month as the new target. Made it.

Then $50,000. Made it.

Then something changed.

---

## The Crossing

The thing that changed was Convertkit finding its people.

The blogger and creator community was not a market that Madison Avenue had identified and targeted. It was a scattered, self-organized collection of people who were figuring out how to make money from the audiences they'd built. They talked to each other. They recommended tools to each other.

When ConvertKit started appearing in the recommendations of prominent bloggers — when Pat Flynn at Smart Passive Income mentioned it, when other creators with large audiences endorsed it — the growth changed character.

It was no longer grinding. It was compounding.

By 2016, ConvertKit was doing $1 million in annual recurring revenue. By 2017, $2 million. By 2020, $20 million.

Nathan Barry had not changed the product fundamentally. He had not cracked a new insight or pivoted to a new market. He had found the right people, built their trust slowly, and let them tell each other.

---

## What Patience Looks Like

Nathan Barry is frequently cited in conversations about the "long game" in building software.

He spent years building something that looked, by external metrics, like it wasn't working. He had a product that competed with well-funded incumbents. He had an audience but not a viral growth engine. He had conviction but not evidence — not yet.

What he had was a specific point of view about who he was building for and why the existing tools failed them. He held that point of view when it would have been easier to build something more generic for a larger market.

The creators vindicated him. Not because he was lucky — because he was right.

---

## The Last Thing

Nathan Barry still runs ConvertKit. He took the company partially remote before it was fashionable. He is candid about the near-failures, the $1,500/month moments, the times he almost quit.

He tells the story of building it not because it's a success story in the traditional sense. He tells it because it's a patience story — a story about the gap between having a real insight and having the evidence to prove it.

"Everyone builds for growth," he has said. "I built for the long game."

The long game paid out.

It usually does, for the people willing to stay in it.
