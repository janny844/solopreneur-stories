# Simon Høiberg: The Danish Developer Who Made 9-to-5 Optional

Simon Høiberg had a stable job as a software developer in Denmark.

Good salary. Reasonable hours. The kind of employment that makes responsible adults nod approvingly.

He was bored out of his mind.

Not because the work was bad — because it was finite. He could see exactly where the job led: senior developer, then maybe a team lead role, then management, then retirement. The path was clear and the path was someone else's.

He started building things at night.

---

## The First Products

Simon Høiberg's early products followed a pattern that many developer-founders recognize: technically interesting, commercially unsuccessful.

He built a language learning app. He built a developer tool. He built a few things that worked fine and made almost no money.

He was learning, though not what he thought he was learning. He thought he was learning how to build products. He was actually learning that building products and finding customers for products are completely different skills, and he only had one of them.

The lesson arrived when a small app he had built — almost as an afterthought — started generating $200/month without any marketing effort. He had built it to scratch his own itch, shared it in one Reddit thread, and forgotten about it.

The $200/month was not from his best work. It was from his most useful work.

The distinction changed how he built.

---

## The Strategy

Simon Høiberg's strategy, once he articulated it, was simple: find niche problems that developers and small businesses encountered repeatedly, build the smallest possible solution, charge a small monthly fee, repeat.

Each product was a small bet. A small bet that might fail — and often did. But each failure was cheap, because the products were small, and each success compounded.

He started documenting the strategy on YouTube. Not as a guru, not as someone who had "made it" — as someone actively trying to figure it out, sharing what worked and what didn't with specificity that most YouTube creators avoided because specificity meant being wrong in public.

His YouTube channel grew because the content was honest.

---

## Teaching as Distribution

Simon Høiberg's YouTube channel — eventually reaching hundreds of thousands of subscribers — was not a separate business from his product portfolio. It was the distribution.

When he launched a new product, the channel provided instant access to an audience of people who trusted him because they had watched him figure things out honestly. Not every viewer became a customer. But a meaningful percentage did, and that percentage was enough to make new products viable in a way they wouldn't have been without the channel.

He had built what the marketing world calls a "media-first business" without setting out to. He built it because he kept answering questions publicly that other developers were asking privately.

---

## The Exit From Employment

Simon Høiberg left his job in 2020.

Not dramatically — his products had been generating enough revenue for long enough that the decision was financially clear long before he made it psychologically. He had built the floor high enough that employment was optional.

That optionality — the ability to leave not because he hated his job but because he had built something better — is the outcome he talks about most when asked what the goal was.

"I didn't want to quit my job because I hated it," he has said. "I wanted to quit because I had something I liked more."

That's the difference. Quitting from hate is escape. Quitting from better is choice.

---

## The Last Thing

Simon Høiberg made 9-to-5 optional.

Not by striking it rich on a single product. By building many small things that worked, documenting the process for people who wanted to do the same, and compounding both the revenue and the audience over years.

The Danish developer who was bored at work is now building things that interest him, teaching what he learns, and choosing every morning whether to work or not.

He almost always chooses to work.

But the choice is his.
