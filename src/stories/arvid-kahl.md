# Arvid Kahl: The Developer Who Sold His SaaS and Wrote the Book on How He Did It

The wire transfer confirmed at 11:23 AM on a Tuesday.

Arvid Kahl had just sold FeedbackPanda — a SaaS he had built with his partner — for an amount he wouldn't disclose publicly but that was, he has said, "life-changing."

He was thirty-five years old. He had been building the company for less than two years.

He closed his laptop and went for a walk. He did not know what to do with his hands.

---

## The Problem That Became a Product

FeedbackPanda started as a tool Arvid's partner needed.

She was an online English teacher — one of thousands of contractors working for Chinese EdTech companies, providing one-on-one English lessons to Chinese children via video call. The work was good but the administrative burden was crushing: after every lesson, teachers were required to write detailed feedback reports for each student's parents.

Fifty lessons a week. Fifty feedback reports. Each one written from scratch.

She spent hours every week on feedback that the parents barely read. Arvid, watching this, did what developers do: he automated it.

FeedbackPanda was a template engine. Teachers could save sentence patterns, fill in student-specific details, and generate professional feedback in seconds instead of minutes. The time saving was not incremental — it was transformational. Teachers who had spent three hours a week on feedback now spent fifteen minutes.

He launched it to the community of online teachers. In the first month, he made $120.

In the second month, $800.

In the twelfth month, $30,000.

---

## The Unexpected Moat

What Arvid Kahl understood — slowly, then completely — was that FeedbackPanda had a moat he hadn't planned for: community.

Online English teachers were a tight-knit, self-organized group. They had Facebook groups, forums, referral networks. When one teacher found something that made their job easier, they told ten other teachers. When ten teachers loved it, a hundred followed.

He did not advertise. He did not run campaigns. He showed up in the communities, helped people, answered questions, and let the product speak through the teachers who used it.

"The best distribution channel for a B2C SaaS," he wrote later, "is the community that already exists around the problem you're solving."

He had not created the community. He had found it, built something it needed, and then earned its trust by showing up consistently.

---

## The Sale

When the EdTech market shifted — when the Chinese government cracked down on private tutoring companies in 2021, devastating the industry — Arvid had already sold FeedbackPanda in 2019.

The timing was lucky. But the decision to sell was deliberate.

He and his partner had built something real. They were at the point where growing it further would require either raising capital or hiring a team — both of which would change the nature of what they'd built and how they lived. They liked what they'd built. They liked how they lived. They sold.

The acquiring company could scale it. They would use the proceeds to figure out what came next.

---

## What Came Next

What came next was a book.

Arvid Kahl spent two years writing *The Embedded Entrepreneur* — a systematic guide to finding, validating, and building products for communities. Then a second book. Then a newsletter, *The Bootstrapped Founder*, that grew to tens of thousands of readers.

The books were not passive income in the way people use that phrase dismissively. They were the result of hundreds of hours of writing, editing, thinking, revising. But they were also, once written, assets that generated revenue indefinitely.

By 2023, between the books, the newsletter, consulting, and speaking, Arvid Kahl was generating meaningful income from his writing alone — having never raised a dollar of venture capital, having built and sold one product and then pivoted entirely to teaching others how to do what he'd done.

---

## The Lesson That Travels

Arvid Kahl's core teaching is deceptively simple: before you build, find the community.

Not the market. The community.

A market is an abstraction. A community is people who already talk to each other, already have opinions, already have problems they're actively trying to solve. Build for them and they will tell each other. Build for a market and you'll spend everything you have on acquisition.

He found the community of online teachers because his partner was one of them. He didn't have to research the pain. He lived next to it.

That proximity — that inside knowledge — was the unfair advantage that no amount of money could replicate.

---

## The Wire Transfer

The wire transfer that confirmed at 11:23 AM on a Tuesday was the end of one story and the beginning of another.

He had built something real with someone he loved and sold it before the market that made it possible disappeared entirely.

He went for a walk. He did not know what to do with his hands.

Eventually he went home, opened his laptop, and started writing.

Some people don't know when to stop. Arvid Kahl knew exactly when to start again.
