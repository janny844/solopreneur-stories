# Jon Yongfook: The Designer Who Built an API for Screenshots

Jon Yongfook had failed seven times before Bannerbear.

Not metaphorical failures. Seven actual products, built, launched, and abandoned over eight years. Some made a little money. Most made none. All of them taught him something he would eventually use.

The eighth product changed his life. He nearly didn't build it.

---

## The Accumulated Weight of Failure

Jon Yongfook is Japanese-British. He grew up in the UK, studied design, moved to Tokyo, built things, moved to Singapore, built more things.

Each product was real work. Real months of effort. Real launches on Hacker News or Product Hunt, with real user feedback and real revenue numbers that started with zero and often ended close to it.

What kept him going was not optimism exactly — he had burned through most of that by product number four. What kept him going was something more durable: curiosity about the craft. He genuinely liked building things. He liked the problem-solving, the design decisions, the moment when something that had been broken started working.

The business part was frustrating. The building part was not.

"I kept going not because I believed the next one would work," he said in an interview. "I kept going because I kept finding new problems I wanted to solve."

---

## The Problem He Kept Running Into

Bannerbear started with a repetitive annoyance.

Jon was building a travel app. The app needed to auto-generate social media images — open graph images, Twitter cards, Facebook previews. For every piece of content, the app needed to create a custom image with the right text and visuals overlaid.

He built this image generation system. It took weeks. It was complex. It worked.

He thought: other people must be doing this constantly. Other developers must be building this same system, over and over, for different products. There must be a better way.

He built the better way and called it Bannerbear.

Bannerbear was an API. Developers could send it a template and some data, and it would return a generated image. That's it. One thing, done well.

---

## The Launch Nobody Expected

He launched Bannerbear on Hacker News with a brief post: "I built an API for auto-generating social images. Feedback welcome."

He had launched on Hacker News before. Usually he got a handful of upvotes and some constructive criticism before the thread disappeared.

This time it hit the front page.

He watched the signups come in over the following hours — not a flood, but a steady stream of developers who had exactly the problem he was solving and had been solving it the hard way.

First month: $800 in MRR.

Second month: $2,200.

Sixth month: $10,000.

He had never crossed $10,000 MRR with any of his previous products. He crossed it on product number eight.

---

## What Eight Failures Taught Him

Jon Yongfook talks about failure differently from most founders who have had it.

He doesn't talk about it as a dark period you push through. He talks about it as an education you pay for with time instead of money.

Each of his seven previous products taught him something specific: Product 1 taught him that "build it and they will come" is false. Product 3 taught him that B2C without a distribution strategy is a dead end. Product 5 taught him that charging from day one is better than building a free user base and hoping to monetize later. Product 6 taught him that APIs are more defensible than consumer apps because the switching cost is higher.

He applied every lesson to Bannerbear.

"By the time I built the thing that worked," he said, "I had already solved most of the non-technical problems. The technical ones were the easy part."

---

## The Life He Built

Bannerbear became a lifestyle business before it became a SaaS success story — but both things are true.

Jon ran it from Bali for a while, then from Tokyo, then from various cities. He hired one contractor. He kept the product focused. He raised prices methodically as the value became clear.

By 2022, Bannerbear was generating over $300,000 in annual recurring revenue.

One person. An API. A problem he kept running into until he finally solved it.

---

## The Last Thing

Seven failures and one product that worked. The math sounds bad until you understand what the seven failures were: they were the tuition for the education that made product eight possible.

Jon Yongfook did not get lucky on product eight. He got skilled, slowly, on products one through seven, and then applied that skill to a real problem at the right time.

The eighth attempt is not the same as the first. It carries everything the first seven taught.

That's why founders who keep going find it eventually. They're not playing the same game each time. They're playing a different game with better equipment.
