# Ben Tossell: The Non-Coder Who Built a No-Code Empire

Ben Tossell could not code.

This was, for years, the fact that defined his relationship with the technology industry. He worked at Product Hunt. He was surrounded by builders. He watched people ship products constantly and could not participate.

He tried to learn. He did the tutorials. It did not stick — not because he was incapable, but because the gap between "I understand a for-loop" and "I can build the thing in my head" was so wide that motivation died in the middle of it.

Then he discovered the tools.

---

## The No-Code Realization

Around 2018, a category of software was maturing that made it possible to build real applications without writing code: Bubble, Webflow, Zapier, Airtable, Integromat.

Ben Tossell started using them and discovered he could build things.

Not toy things. Real things. Marketplaces, directories, internal tools, automated workflows. Things that would have required a developer and a budget six months earlier.

The realization was not just personal. It was that a very large group of people — everyone with an idea and no engineering background — had just been handed the ability to build.

Nobody was teaching them.

---

## Makerpad

Ben Tossell launched Makerpad in 2019 as a library of tutorials for building without code.

The format was specific: not "learn Bubble," but "build an Airbnb clone," "build a job board," "automate your invoicing." Outcome-oriented, project-based, showing the actual clicks.

It was a membership. People paid monthly for access.

It grew quickly, because the audience had no alternative. Traditional coding education was aimed at people becoming developers. Makerpad was aimed at people who wanted a specific thing to exist and did not care about becoming anything.

---

## The Community Effect

Makerpad became more than tutorials.

The community — a Slack, then a broader network — filled with people who had built real businesses on no-code tools. They shared what worked. They hired each other. They validated each other's approach at a time when the broader tech industry treated no-code as a toy.

That validation was the real product.

Being a non-technical founder in 2019 meant being told, constantly, that you needed a technical co-founder. Makerpad's community was thousands of people proving that was no longer true.

---

## The Zapier Acquisition

In 2021, Zapier acquired Makerpad.

The strategic logic was clear: Zapier was infrastructure for exactly the audience Makerpad had assembled and educated. The acquisition brought both the content library and the community into Zapier's orbit.

Ben Tossell — the guy who couldn't code — sold his company to one of the most important automation platforms in the industry.

---

## Ben's Bites

After Makerpad, Ben Tossell did it again, in a different domain.

When large language models started becoming broadly useful in late 2022, he launched Ben's Bites — a daily newsletter about AI.

The playbook was identical to Makerpad: identify a technology that is about to matter enormously, notice that non-experts have no accessible on-ramp, build the on-ramp, build the community, monetize the attention.

Ben's Bites grew to over 100,000 subscribers within months and became one of the most-read AI newsletters in existence.

He had proven the method was repeatable.

---

## The Last Thing

Ben Tossell could not code, and for years that felt like a permanent limitation.

It turned out to be his qualification.

He built the definitive resource for non-coders because he was one — because he knew exactly which explanations landed and which assumed knowledge his audience didn't have.

The developers who understood the tools better could not have built Makerpad.

They had forgotten what not knowing feels like.
