# Nicolas Cole: The World of Warcraft Addict Who Became a Ghostwriting Millionaire

Nicolas Cole spent his teenage years as one of the top-ranked World of Warcraft players in North America.

Not casually. Obsessively. Thousands of hours. The kind of commitment that concerned his parents and impressed exactly nobody outside the game.

He was also, during those same years, writing constantly — forum posts, guides, strategy breakdowns for other players. Tens of thousands of words that nobody thought of as writing because it was on a gaming forum.

He was building a skill without knowing it.

---

## Quora

In 2014, Nicolas Cole started answering questions on Quora.

The topic didn't matter much at first. He answered questions about fitness, about gaming, about growing up, about bodybuilding. He wrote in a distinctive style — short paragraphs, personal narrative, specific detail, an emotional beat in the middle.

His answers got read. Then they got read a lot. Then Inc. and Time and Business Insider started republishing them.

Within two years, his Quora answers had been read over 50 million times.

He had accidentally discovered a fact about the internet that would define his career: the distribution channel is where the readers are, and the writing style that wins is the one adapted to the channel, not the one you learned in school.

---

## The Ghostwriting Business

Nicolas Cole's readership attracted a specific kind of inbound: executives and founders who wanted to build audiences the way he had, but couldn't write.

He started ghostwriting.

The first clients paid a few thousand dollars per month. The later clients paid tens of thousands. Because what he was selling was not typing — it was the ability to take a busy executive's half-formed ideas and turn them into content that people actually read.

That skill was rare. He priced accordingly.

---

## Digital Press and Ship 30 for 30

Nicolas Cole eventually built two businesses on top of the ghostwriting insight.

**Digital Press** — a ghostwriting agency, systematized. He built processes, trained writers, and turned his personal skill into a scalable service. Multi-million-dollar revenue.

**Ship 30 for 30** — a cohort-based course, co-founded with Dickie Bush, teaching people to write online in thirty days by publishing thirty short "atomic essays."

Ship 30 for 30 was the more interesting product. It solved the actual problem most aspiring writers have, which is not craft but consistency. The course made you publish daily for thirty days with a cohort watching. That constraint produced more writers than any craft-focused curriculum.

Ship 30 for 30 generated millions in revenue across dozens of cohorts.

---

## The Frameworks

Nicolas Cole's contribution to writing education is that he treats writing as engineering rather than art.

He has published frameworks with names: the "1-3-1" structure, the "Curiosity Gap," the "What/Why/How" formula. Each is a template — a repeatable structure that produces readable writing without requiring inspiration.

Purists object. Cole's answer is that art and craft are different things, and most people who want to write online need craft first. The art can come later, once the mechanics stop consuming all available attention.

He is probably right, judging by the number of people who have gone from never publishing to publishing consistently after taking his courses.

## The Last Thing

Nicolas Cole spent his teenage years as a World of Warcraft addict writing strategy guides nobody credited as writing.

He was, it turned out, practicing.

Ten thousand hours of forum posts became the fastest, most channel-native writer on Quora, which became a ghostwriting business, which became an agency, which became a writing school that has taught tens of thousands of people to publish.

The thing his parents worried about turned out to be the training.

That happens more often than anyone admits.
