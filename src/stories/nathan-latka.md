# Nathan Latka: The College Dropout Who Built a Database of Other People's Revenue

Nathan Latka's first business was a Facebook app.

He was nineteen, at Virginia Tech, and he built a tool that let brands design their Facebook pages without a developer. He called it Heyo.

It grew to over $5 million in revenue while he was still in college. He dropped out. He ran it for a few years. He sold it.

He was twenty-three and looking for what came next.

---

## The Podcast Nobody Wanted to Be On

Nathan Latka started a podcast with a premise other podcasters considered rude.

He would interview SaaS founders and ask, directly, for their exact numbers. Revenue. Growth rate. Churn. Customer count. Team size. Amount raised. Valuation.

Not "what's your revenue range." The actual number.

Most business podcasts danced around this. The convention was that these were private matters, that founders would refuse, that asking was gauche.

Nathan Latka asked anyway, on every episode, without apologizing for asking.

An extraordinary number of founders answered.

---

## The Database

The podcast was not really the product. The data was.

Every interview added a data point: a company, a revenue figure, a growth rate, a date. After a thousand episodes, Nathan Latka had a proprietary dataset on private SaaS company financials that did not exist anywhere else.

He turned it into Latka.com — a subscription database used by investors, acquirers, competitors, and founders benchmarking themselves.

The podcast was the collection mechanism. The database was the business.

This is one of the more elegant business designs in modern media: a content product whose exhaust is a data product worth more than the content.

---

## The Personality Cost

Nathan Latka is a polarizing figure.

His style is aggressive. The questions are blunt. He interrupts. He has been accused of prioritizing spectacle over substance. Some founders have said afterward that they regretted disclosing what they disclosed.

He does not appear to mind the criticism.

His argument is that the information asymmetry in private markets serves incumbents and investors at the expense of founders — that a founder who doesn't know what a comparable company's revenue multiple is will be underpaid in an acquisition, and that the polite convention of secrecy is not neutral.

Whether or not you accept the argument, the dataset he built is real and useful and did not exist before he was rude enough to ask.

---

## The Acquisition Strategy

Nathan Latka eventually did something predictable in retrospect: he started buying software companies.

He had, after all, built a database that told him exactly which companies were undervalued, which were growing, which had founders who sounded exhausted on a podcast recording.

He raised a fund. He made acquisitions. He applied the data.

The information advantage he had accumulated over years of interviews turned into an investing advantage.

---

## The Last Thing

Nathan Latka built a $5 million business in college, sold it, and then spent years asking people a question everyone else was too polite to ask.

"What's your revenue?"

A thousand times. On the record.

The politeness convention existed because it served the people who benefited from secrecy. He broke it, took the criticism, and ended up owning the most complete picture of private SaaS financials in existence.

Sometimes the moat is just being willing to ask.
