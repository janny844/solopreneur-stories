# Peter Askew: The Man Who Bought a Domain Name and Accidentally Sold Vidalia Onions

Peter Askew bought a domain name.

The domain was `vidaliaonions.com`. He paid a few thousand dollars for it at auction, on instinct, without a business plan.

He was a domain investor. Buying names was what he did. Most of them sat idle, occasionally sold, occasionally didn't.

But this one bothered him. It was too good to sit idle. Vidalia onions are a real, protected, geographically-specific agricultural product from Georgia, with a genuine season and genuine fans.

Somebody should be selling them at that domain.

So he drove to Georgia and found a farmer.

---

## The Cold Call to a Farm

Peter Askew had no experience in agriculture, logistics, food safety, or perishable shipping.

He drove to Vidalia, Georgia, and started asking around for a farmer who might be interested in an internet partnership.

The reasonable expectation is that this goes nowhere. A stranger from out of state shows up asking about selling onions online.

He found a farmer. They made a deal. The farmer would grow and ship. Peter would run the website, handle the marketing, and manage the orders.

The first season worked.

---

## The Economics of an Absurd Business

The Vidalia onion business is a spectacular example of the value of an exact-match domain.

When onion season arrives and someone searches for "vidalia onions," Peter Askew's site is the answer. Not because of an SEO agency. Because the domain is literally the search term and the site sells the thing.

Customer acquisition cost: approximately zero.

The business generates several hundred thousand dollars per season, concentrated in a few months of the year, with a business model that consists of: own the right domain, partner with the right producer, don't screw up the shipping.

---

## Deep South Rides and Other Absurdities

Peter Askew's other businesses follow the same logic, and each is stranger than the last.

He owns and operates businesses built around domains he acquired: a horse-riding directory, a dude-ranch booking site, various niche properties in categories nobody would deliberately choose.

He calls the approach "domain-driven entrepreneurship" — starting with the asset and then discovering what business it implies, rather than starting with a business idea and then acquiring assets.

It is completely backwards from every startup methodology.

It works because search intent is a real, measurable, monetizable thing, and owning the exact phrase that expresses an intent is a durable advantage that nobody can copy.

---

## Why This Story Matters

Peter Askew's story is included in this book because it breaks the pattern of every other story in it.

He did not have a personal pain point. He was not scratching his own itch. He had never grown an onion, ridden a horse professionally, or worked in agriculture.

He had an asset and a willingness to drive to Georgia and knock on doors.

The lesson is not "buy domains." The lesson is that opportunity is not always found by introspection. Sometimes it is found by noticing that a valuable thing is sitting unused and being the person willing to do the unglamorous logistics of putting it to work.

---

## The Last Thing

Peter Askew bought a domain name about onions because it seemed like a waste to leave it parked.

Then he drove to Georgia and found a farmer.

Everything else — the seasons, the revenue, the strange portfolio of businesses about horses and produce — followed from a man who could not tolerate the idea of a perfectly good domain name doing nothing.

He is the only person in this book whose entire career began with an aesthetic objection to waste.
